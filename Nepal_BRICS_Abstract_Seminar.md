## Abstract

Nepal's position as a landlocked Himalayan state sandwiched between two BRICS members — India and China — provides a unique analytical lens through which to examine small state agency in resource diplomacy. This article investigates the central research question: **How has Nepal strategically navigated BRICS resource diplomacy to advance its national development objectives between 2015 and 2025?**

Employing a qualitative case study methodology supplemented by secondary data from government reports, multilateral institutional documents, and academic literature, this study analyses Nepal's engagement across four dimensions: hydropower diplomacy, multilateral financing through the New Development Bank (NDB), Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) connectivity projects, and South-South cooperation frameworks.

The findings reveal that Nepal has adopted a **"competitive leveraging" strategy** — deliberately cultivating competing BRICS interests in its hydropower sector and transit corridors to negotiate better project terms, financing conditions, and technology transfers. Specific case studies including the Arun-3 Hydropower Project (India, 900 MW), the Budhi Gandaki Project (China, 1,200 MW), and BRI-linked Kerung-Kathmandu railway negotiations demonstrate both the opportunities and structural limitations of this approach.

The paper identifies a significant research gap in existing scholarship: while bilateral Nepal–India and Nepal–China relations are well-documented, the *multilateral* dimension of Nepal's BRICS engagement and Nepal's proactive agency therein remain virtually unexamined. This paper contributes to the nascent literature on small state resource diplomacy in Asia, extends BRICS scholarship beyond Africa-centric cases, and offers actionable policy frameworks for Nepalese development planners and Multinational Enterprises (MNEs) operating in the Himalayan resource sector.

Theoretical contributions include a novel **"Himalayan Balancing Model" (HBM)** — a four-component framework of resource niche identification, competitive leveraging, multilateral anchoring, and incremental commitment — applicable to other small, resource-rich states navigating great power competition.
