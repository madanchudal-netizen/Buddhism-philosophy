# An Eastern Philosophical Approach to Restore National Integrity: Resolving Societal Disputes in Modern Nepal

## An Integrative Framework of Nepalese Indigenous, Hindu, Buddhist, Chinese, and Civilizational-State Thought

---

## Abstract

Modern Nepal is experiencing acute societal fragmentation arising from ethnic federalism, ideological polarization, secular-religious contestation, and the uncritical transplantation of Western liberal-democratic and Marxist frameworks. These imported models, predicated on binary opposition, possessive individualism, and class antagonism, have often intensified rather than healed the nation’s identity-based cleavages. This article proposes an indigenous Eastern philosophical framework for restoring social harmony and national integrity by synthesizing the six orthodox Hindu *Astika Darshanas* (*Samkhya*, *Yoga*, *Nyaya*, *Vaisheshika*, *Mimamsa*, and *Vedanta*), the heterodox Buddhist insights of *Pratītyasamutpāda* and *Madhyamaka*, and the Chinese traditions of Confucianism and Daoism. It further integrates distinctive Nepalese and Himalayan philosophical archives—*Shaiva* and *Pashupata* traditions, the *Upanishads*, the *Ashtavakra Gita* (rooted in the Janaka-Mithila heritage), Himalayan *Bon* cosmology, the Kirat *Mundhum*, and indigenous animist-shamanic institutions—into a coherent civilizational paradigm. Drawing on Alexander Dugin’s Fourth Political Theory, the article argues that Nepal should be reconceived as a multi-polar *Civilization-State* grounded in its own *Dasein* rather than as a derivative nation-state within a liberal unipole. Through qualitative desk research and comparative philosophical analysis, the study develops concrete policy recommendations for constitutional design, education, governance, and community reconciliation. The findings indicate that a plural yet unified civilizational identity—founded on non-duality, interdependence, moral duty, ritual reciprocity, and ecological sacredness—offers Nepal a more authentic and sustainable path to national integrity.

**Keywords:** Nepal, national integrity, Shaivism, Kirat Mundhum, Himalayan Bon, Ashtavakra Gita, Upanishads, Advaita Vedanta, Madhyamaka, Fourth Political Theory, civilizational identity, indigenous philosophy.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Nepal’s Civilizational Matrix and Contemporary Fragmentation

Situated at the cultural and ecological interface of the Indian plains, the Tibetan plateau, and the Himalayan highlands, Nepal has historically functioned as a civilizational crossroads. Its social fabric is woven from multiple spiritual lineages: the *Shaiva* and *Vedantic* traditions of the Kathmandu Valley, the *Buddhist* monasticism of the Himalayan borderlands, the *Bon* and animist-shamanic practices of Tibeto-Burman communities, the *Mundhum* oral cosmology of the Kirat peoples, the Mithila philosophical heritage associated with King Janak, and the localized sacred geographies of countless valleys, rivers, and mountains. This plurality was not, for most of Nepalese history, experienced as a problem to be managed by imported theories; rather, it constituted a lived civilizational unity organized around ritual reciprocity, sacred kingship, caste and ethnic complementarity, and a shared Himalayan cosmology (Slusser, 1982; Levy, 1990).

The contemporary period, however, has witnessed a deep fracturing of this civilizational coherence. The abolition of the Hindu monarchy in 2008, the declaration of secularism, the adoption of ethnic federalism, and the aftermath of the decade-long Maoist insurgency have together produced a political field marked by zero-sum competition among identities. Movements for Madhesi autonomy, Limbuwan, Tharuhat, and various Janajati demands have challenged the unitary nation-state, while Hindu nationalist organizations have resisted secularization and federal restructuring (Hangen, 2010; Lawoti & Hangen, 2013). These disputes are not merely administrative; they are ontological contests over the meaning of Nepal itself.

### 1.2 The Limits of Imported Western Frameworks

The two dominant Western frameworks deployed to interpret and manage these disputes—liberal democracy and Marxism—have both proven inadequate. Liberalism, with its emphasis on individual rights, procedural equality, and interest-group pluralism, introduces a language of competitive claims that tends to fragment organic social bonds into adversarial legal categories. In Nepal, where personhood has traditionally been constituted through kinship, caste, *guthi* obligations, and ritual roles, the reduction of citizenship to atomized rights-bearing individualism has often undermined the very solidarities it claims to protect (Gellner, 2001; Mishra, 1997).

Marxism, particularly in its Maoist form, offered a powerful critique of feudal land relations, caste hierarchy, and regional underdevelopment. Yet its dialectical method presupposes an irreconcilable contradiction between oppressor and oppressed, thereby legitimizing violence as a historically necessary instrument of liberation. Although the Comprehensive Peace Agreement of 2006 formally ended armed conflict, the discursive habit of binary friend-enemy thinking continues to impede consensus-building (Thapa, 2003; Hutt, 2004).

Both frameworks are, in essence, Western parochialisms universalized as global theory. They lack the conceptual vocabulary for *Dharma*, *harmony*, *interdependence*, *non-duality*, and *sacred duty*—categories that remain central to Nepalese civilizational self-understanding.

### 1.3 The Indigenous Philosophical Archive: Rationale and Objectives

This article argues that Nepal possesses an extraordinarily rich indigenous philosophical archive capable of addressing its current crises from within its own civilizational horizon. Existing scholarship on Nepalese conflict has been dominated by political science, sociology, and development studies, with insufficient attention to classical and indigenous philosophy. The present study seeks to fill that gap by integrating the following bodies of thought into a unified analytical framework:

1. The six Hindu *Astika Darshanas*;
2. The *Upanishadic* foundation of Vedantic ethics;
3. The *Ashtavakra Gita* and the royal yoga of King Janak;
4. *Shaiva* and *Pashupata* traditions of Nepal;
5. Himalayan *Bon* and animist-shamanic cosmologies;
6. The Kirat *Mundhum*;
7. Buddhist *Madhyamaka* and *Pratītyasamutpāda*;
8. Confucian and Daoist statecraft;
9. Alexander Dugin’s Fourth Political Theory as a critical horizon.

The article has three interrelated objectives: (a) to demonstrate that Nepal’s philosophical traditions contain sophisticated resources for conflict resolution and governance; (b) to apply these resources to the specific problems of identity politics, federalism, secularism, and social reconciliation; and (c) to outline a strategic roadmap for policymakers, educators, and community leaders.

### 1.4 Methodology

The study employs qualitative desk research and comparative philosophical analysis. It reads primary philosophical texts—classical Sanskrit, Pali, Chinese, and indigenous oral traditions—alongside secondary scholarship in religious studies, anthropology, and political philosophy. The method is hermeneutic and synthetic: it seeks to interpret traditions in their own terms while demonstrating their contemporary relevance. It also engages critically with Dugin’s Fourth Political Theory, distinguishing Nepal’s non-aligned civilizational pluralism from the geopolitical Eurasianism with which Dugin is often associated.

## 2. Theoretical and Conceptual Framework

### 2.1 The *Astika Darshanas*: Classical Hindu Systems of Order and Knowledge

The six orthodox schools of Indian philosophy provide a sophisticated analytical apparatus for evaluating individual and collective equilibrium, knowledge, duty, and ultimate unity.

#### 2.1.1 *Samkhya*: *Purusha*, *Prakriti*, and the *Trigunas*

*Samkhya* distinguishes between *Purusha* (pure consciousness) and *Prakriti* (material nature). All psychophysical phenomena evolve from *Prakriti* through the interplay of the three *Gunas*: *Sattva* (harmony and illumination), *Rajas* (activity and desire), and *Tamas* (inertia and darkness). Social and political health require a *Sattva*-predominant equilibrium. In Nepal, excessive *Rajas* is visible in the restless agitation of ethnic mobilization and partisan vendetta, while *Tamas* appears in bureaucratic corruption, impunity, and civic apathy. A *Sattvic* polity would be one in which activism is disciplined by wisdom, and governance is transparent, calm, and oriented toward the common good (Chatterjee & Datta, 1984; Radhakrishnan, 1927).

#### 2.1.2 *Yoga*: *Chitta Vritti Nirodha*, *Ahimsa*, and *Satya*

Patañjali defines Yoga as *chitta vritti nirodha*—the cessation of mental fluctuations. Applied to a society traumatized by insurgency, repression, and historical grievance, Yoga suggests that peace must begin with the purification of collective mental formations. The ethical disciplines of *Yama* and *Niyama*—especially *ahimsa* (non-violence) and *satya* (truth)—provide the moral foundation for reconciliation. A Nepali truth-and-reconciliation process grounded in *satya* and *ahimsa* would prioritize restorative justice over retribution (Patañjali, 2009; Bryant, 2009).

#### 2.1.3 *Nyaya* and *Vaisheshika*: Reason, Evidence, and Ontological Precision

*Nyaaya* offers a rigorous theory of knowledge (*Pramana*) including perception, inference, comparison, and authoritative testimony. For governance, this entails evidence-based policymaking, transparent deliberation, and respect for both scientific expertise and traditional knowledge. *Vaisheshika*, with its taxonomy of *Padarthas* (categories of being), encourages precise analysis of social reality—distinguishing caste, class, ethnicity, region, and religion—without reducing one category to another. This non-reductionist realism is essential for conflict management (Sharma, 1997).

#### 2.1.4 *Mimamsa*: *Dharma* as Social Obligation

*Mimamsa* is preeminently concerned with the interpretation of *Dharma* as ritual and moral duty. Socially, it teaches that harmony arises from the performance of one’s duties toward others rather than from the assertion of rights against others. While historical caste *Dharma* was oppressive, a reinterpreted universal *Dharma*—truthfulness, compassion, purity, and self-restraint—can provide an integrative civic ethic for Nepal (Lipner, 2012).

#### 2.1.5 *Vedanta*: *Advaita* and *Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam*

*Advaita Vedanta*, systematized by Ādi Śaṅkara, teaches that apparent multiplicity is grounded in a non-dual absolute consciousness (*Brahman*). Individual and group identities are phenomenally real but ultimately not separate. The *Maha Upanishad* declares *Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam*—“the world is one family”—thereby providing a cosmopolitan yet rooted vision of unity. For Nepal, *Advaita* implies that ethnic and religious diversity can be celebrated precisely because all identities are expressions of a deeper common Self (Śaṅkara, 1978; Olivelle, 1992, 1996).

### 2.2 Upanishadic Foundations of Civilizational Ethics

The *Upanishads* constitute the philosophical core of the Hindu tradition and provide crucial resources for reimagining Nepalese national integrity. The *Isha Upanishad* integrates inner renunciation with outer action, teaching that a ruler or citizen can participate fully in the world while recognizing its transcendence. The *Katha Upanishad* distinguishes between temporary pleasure and lasting wisdom, warning that societies oriented solely toward consumption and power lose their spiritual bearings. The *Mundaka Upanishad* distinguishes lower knowledge (technical skill) from higher knowledge (Self-knowledge), suggesting that education must cultivate ethical and metaphysical insight alongside material competence.

The *Brihadaranyaka Upanishad* famously places King Janak of Videha at the center of a philosophical assembly; the king is both sovereign and seeker, exemplifying the ideal of the philosopher-ruler. This is directly relevant to Nepal, since the Mithila region associated with Janak lies within Nepal’s Terai and the city of Janakpur remains a major pilgrimage site. Finally, the *Chandogya Upanishad*’s teaching *Tat Tvam Asi*—“That Thou Art”—declares the identity of the individual self with the universal Self, providing a metaphysical basis for non-discrimination and social solidarity (Olivelle, 1996).

### 2.3 The *Ashtavakra Gita*: King Janak and the Royal Yoga of Non-Dual Detachment

The *Ashtavakra Gita* presents a dialogue between King Janak of Videha and the sage Ashtavakra. Unlike the *Bhagavad Gita*, which addresses action in the world, the *Ashtavakra Gita* radicalizes non-dualism: the Self is pure awareness, untouched by body, mind, social role, or historical circumstance. It teaches *vairagya* (dispassion), *nirveda* (equanimity), and the transcendence of ego-identification (Ashtavakra, 1990; Ashtavakra, 2001).

For Nepal, Janak is a doubly significant figure. As the philosopher-king of Mithila, he represents the possibility that governance can be grounded in Self-knowledge rather than in personal ambition or partisan interest. As a figure linked to Janakpur and to the *Ramayana* narrative, he is embedded in Nepalese civilizational memory. The *Ashtavakra Gita* therefore offers a model of leadership in which the ruler is detached from the fruits of power, capable of just governance because not imprisoned by caste, ethnicity, or ideology. Such leadership is urgently needed in a polity where politicians frequently instrumentalize identity for electoral gain.

### 2.4 Shaivism: *Pashupata*, Tantric, and Himalayan Non-Dualism

Nepal has been historically identified as a *Shaiva* kingdom, with Pashupatinath—Lord of all beings—serving as the national deity. *Pashupata Shaivism*, one of the earliest organized Shaiva sects, understands all beings (*pashus*) as bound souls under the grace of the Lord (*Pashupati*). This theology contains an implicit social egalitarianism: before the divine, all beings are equally dependent and equally capable of liberation. The annual pilgrimage to Pashupatinath by Hindus and Buddhists alike, and the sharing of ritual space by diverse castes and ethnicities, testify to the integrative potential of this tradition (Sanderson, 1988; Lorenzen, 1972).

Tantric Shaivism—whether in its Kashmiri *Pratyabhijna* form or its Nepalese ritual expressions—goes further. It affirms that the entire universe is the self-expression of Shiva-Shakti and that every individual consciousness is ultimately Shiva. The Tantric concept of *pratyabhijna* (recognition) means recognizing the divine in oneself and others. This has profound implications for social hierarchy: if every person is Shiva, then caste, ethnic, and gender hierarchies cannot be metaphysically ultimate. Slusser (1982) and Levy (1990) have documented how Newar Shaiva-Buddhist ritual culture in the Kathmandu Valley created complex but integrated civic spaces. A contemporary Nepalese politics informed by Shaiva non-dualism would ground dignity and equality in sacred ontology rather than in abstract legal formalism.

### 2.5 Bon and Himalayan Animist-Shamanic Philosophy

The Himalayan regions of Nepal—Mustang, Dolpo, Humla, Solu-Khumbu, Tamang areas, and Gurung regions—have been deeply shaped by *Bon*, the pre-Buddhist religious complex of Tibet, as well as by localized animist-shamanic traditions. Bon cosmology posits a multi-layered universe of gods, ancestors, nature spirits, and demonic forces, and it emphasizes ritual reciprocity, healing, and the restoration of cosmic balance (Kvaerne, 1995; Mumford, 1989; Samuel, 1993).

Key Bon concepts include the three worlds (upper, middle, lower), the cult of ancestors (*dralha*), and the propitiation of local deities associated with mountains, lakes, and forests. These ideas are not merely theological; they constitute an ecological philosophy in which human communities are embedded within a sacred landscape. In Nepal, similar ideas are found in Tamang *bon* shamanism, Gurung *pae* rituals, Rai *mundum* practices, and Magar *dhami-jhankri* healing traditions. Conflicts over land, water, and territory in Nepal are therefore never purely economic; they violate cosmological relationships.

Bon and animist-shamanic thought offer three resources for national integrity. First, they provide models of conflict resolution through ritual mediation and restoration rather than adversarial punishment. Second, they ground environmental ethics in sacred geography, making ecological conservation a matter of spiritual duty. Third, they affirm the dignity of indigenous knowledge systems that have often been marginalized by modernist development paradigms (Namkhai Norbu, 1995).

### 2.6 Kirat Mundhum: The Oral-Philosophical Tradition of Eastern Nepal

The *Mundhum* is the sacred oral tradition of the Kirat peoples—Rai, Limbu, Sunuwar, Yakkha, and others—of Eastern Nepal. Though diverse across clans, Mundhum traditions share a coherent cosmology, ethical code, and social vision. They narrate the creation of the world, the origin of human beings, the migrations of ancestors, the structure of the cosmos, and the proper relations among humans, ancestors, spirits, and nature (Chemjong, 2003; Subba, 1999; Gaenszle, 1991, 2000).

Among the Limbu, the supreme being is *Tagera Ningwaphuma*; among various Rai groups, the primordial couple *Sumnima* and *Paruhang* feature centrally. Mundhum morality emphasizes hospitality, honesty, respect for elders, communal solidarity, reciprocity, and the protection of forests, water sources, and sacred groves. Ritual specialists—*phedangma*, *bijuwa*, *yebang*, *samba*—preserve and transmit this knowledge through oral performance.

The *Mundhum* also contains institutions of governance. Village councils of elders, customary laws, and seasonal rituals such as *Sakela* and *Chandi Naach* renew communal bonds and regulate social relations. For Nepal’s federal experiment, the *Mundhum* suggests that autonomy need not mean ethnic separatism; it can mean the recognition of locally embedded moral and ecological communities within a larger civilizational whole.

### 2.7 Buddhist Philosophy: *Pratītyasamutpāda* and *Madhyamaka*

The Buddha’s teaching of *Pratītyasamutpāda* (interdependent co-arising) states that all phenomena arise in dependence upon conditions; nothing possesses an independent, self-subsisting essence. Applied to Nepali society, this undermines the myth of ethnically pure, autonomous communities. Hill and Terai, Hindu and Buddhist, Bahun-Chhetri and Janajati groups have co-constituted one another through centuries of migration, trade, intermarriage, shared ritual, and political alliance (Gethin, 1998; Garfield, 1995).

Nāgārjuna’s *Madhyamaka* rejects metaphysical extremes—essentialism and nihilism, eternalism and annihilationism—and advocates a Middle Way. In political practice, this means avoiding both rigid ethno-nationalism, which reifies identity, and rootless cosmopolitanism, which dissolves meaningful belonging. The Middle Way thus becomes a method for designing federalism, secularism, and development policy that balances autonomy with unity and tradition with modernization (Williams, 2009; Lawrence, 2019).

### 2.8 Chinese Philosophical Traditions: Confucianism and Daoism

Confucianism emphasizes *ren* (benevolence), *li* (ritual propriety), *xiao* (filial piety), and *he* (harmony). Confucian political legitimacy rests on the ruler’s moral virtue, not merely electoral mandate. *He* is not uniformity but the harmonious coordination of differences, like a musical ensemble. For Nepal, this suggests governance in which leaders cultivate ethical character and public ritual embodies respect for both tradition and pluralism (Confucius, 2003; Ivanhoe, 2000).

Daoism offers *wu wei*—effortless action aligned with the *Dao* (natural way). In governance, this means minimizing coercive interference and trusting spontaneous social order. Modern Nepal suffers from top-down policy imposition, foreign conditionalities, and bureaucratic overreach. A Daoist-informed state would exercise restraint, honor local customary institutions, and align development with ecological and cultural rhythms (Miller, 2003; Schwartz, 1985).

### 2.9 Alexander Dugin’s Fourth Political Theory: Civilization-State and *Dasein*

Alexander Dugin’s Fourth Political Theory (4PT) critiques the three modern ideologies—liberalism, communism, and fascism—and especially liberalism’s claim to represent the “end of history.” Dugin argues that liberalism is a particular Western cultural project that has become globally hegemonic through economic, military, and media power. He proposes the *Civilization-State* as an alternative to the nation-state and the globalist unipole, grounding political identity in a people’s distinct *Dasein*—their situated being-in-the-world (Dugin, 2012; Heidegger, 1962).

For Nepal, 4PT provides a language for resisting Western cultural imperialism and reclaiming civilizational autonomy. Nepal is not simply a developing nation-state to be integrated into the liberal world order; it is a distinct civilizational space shaped by Hindu-Buddhist cosmology, Himalayan ecology, and South Asian geopolitics. This article appropriates Dugin critically: Nepal’s civilizational sovereignty should be non-aligned, non-hegemonic, and inwardly reconciliatory, not a vehicle for any external power bloc.

## 3. Critical Analysis and Discussion

### 3.1 Identity beyond Essentialism: *Advaita*, *Mundhum*, and Bon

Nepal’s identity disputes are frequently framed in essentialist terms: Madhesi versus Pahade, Hindu versus secular, federalist versus nationalist. Such framings treat identities as fixed substances rather than as relational processes. *Advaita Vedanta*, *Pratītyasamutpāda*, the *Mundhum*, and Bon cosmology together provide a philosophical corrective.

*Advaita* does not deny the practical reality of diversity; it denies only that diversity constitutes ultimate separateness. Ethnic and religious identities are *mithya*—apparently real but dependent upon a deeper unity. This allows a politics of recognition that does not degenerate into identitarian absolutism. One can affirm Madhesi cultural rights, Limbu autonomy, or Dalit dignity without treating these categories as metaphysically ultimate.

The *Mundhum* offers an indigenous model of identity as ancestral and ecological rather than as purely ethnic-political. Kirat identity is tied to lineage, territory, sacred groves, and seasonal rituals; it is a way of being in the world, not merely a demographic category to be mobilized for elections. Similarly, Bon and Himalayan animism understand identity as embedded in sacred geography and ancestral relationships. When groups understand their identities as co-arisen, relational, and place-based, the logic of competitive victimhood gives way to shared responsibility for the land and its peoples.

### 3.2 Hierarchy, Dignity, and Shaiva Immanence

Caste and ethnic hierarchy remain deeply entrenched in Nepal, despite legal abolition of untouchability and constitutional guarantees of equality. Legal prohibition alone cannot transform social psychology; transformation requires a metaphysical shift. Shaiva theology provides such a shift. If every being is a *pashu* under Pashupati, and if Tantric non-dualism teaches that every consciousness is Shiva, then no caste or ethnicity can claim ontological superiority over another.

This does not imply the erasure of cultural difference. Rather, it implies the sanctification of difference within a higher unity. The Pashupatinath temple complex itself exemplifies this: it is a space where diverse castes, ethnicities, and even Buddhist and Hindu practitioners converge. A national ethics inspired by Shaiva immanence would ground anti-discrimination policy not only in constitutional rights but also in a widely shared spiritual conviction that the divine dwells equally in all.

### 3.3 Governance and Leadership: Janaka, Confucius, and *Ashtavakra*

The crisis of governance in Nepal is partly a crisis of leadership character: corruption, nepotism, populism, and partisan egoism have eroded public trust. The *Ashtavakra Gita* and the Upanishadic figure of King Janak offer an alternative model: the ruler as philosopher, detached from the fruits of power, governed by *satya* and *Dharma*. Such a leader would not instrumentalize ethnicity for electoral gain, because the Self is beyond caste, party, and ambition.

Confucianism complements this by emphasizing ritual propriety, moral self-cultivation, and the responsibility of elites to model virtue for society. A Confucian-inspired civil service ethics—emphasizing public-spiritedness, accountability, and respect for tradition—could complement institutional anti-corruption measures. Together, the Ashtavakran ideal of detached wisdom and the Confucian ideal of moral statecraft point toward a Nepali leadership formation program grounded in classical ethics rather than in mere technical training.

### 3.4 Structural Harmony: *Madhyamaka*, Confucian *He*, and Indigenous Institutions

The Middle Way offers a practical method for policy design. Federal restructuring illustrates the point: centralized unitarism suppressed regional aspirations, while ethnically exclusive provinces risk balkanization. A *Madhyamaka*-informed federalism would create provinces based on economic viability, historical regions, and multi-ethnic coexistence rather than on single-identity enclaves. It would balance provincial autonomy with national unity.

Confucian *he* (harmony) suggests that unity is not uniformity but the coordinated flourishing of differences. Indigenous institutions such as *guthis*, *kipat* land systems, Kirat village councils, and local *dhami-jhankri* mediation networks are practical expressions of this harmony. Daoist *wu wei* counsels the state to support these local institutions rather than to override them with centralized schemes. A structurally harmonious Nepal would thus combine constitutional federalism with customary self-governance, allowing local communities to manage disputes, resources, and rituals according to their own traditions.

### 3.5 Re-establishing Nepal as a Multi-Polar Civilization-State

Dugin’s concept of the *Civilization-State* is especially pertinent for Nepal’s geopolitical self-positioning. Sandwiched between India and China, Nepal has historically balanced these civilizational powers while maintaining distinct religious, linguistic, and political traditions. The modern pressure to choose between Western liberal democracy and various authoritarian models is itself a product of unipolar thinking.

Nepal can instead articulate a multi-polar civilizational identity: a Himalayan-Hindu-Buddhist-Kirat-Bon-Mithila polity that maintains friendly relations with India, China, and the West without subordinating itself to any. This requires rejecting the false binary between “tradition” and “modernity” and recognizing that Nepal’s traditions contain resources for modern self-governance. The Civilization-State model does not imply isolationism; it implies engagement from a position of rooted self-confidence. The Himalayas, Pashupatinath, Lumbini, Janakpur, Kirat sacred groves, and Bon holy mountains are not tourist commodities but ontological anchors of Nepali *Dasein*.

## 4. Strategic Roadmap and Policy Recommendations

### 4.1 Constitutional and Institutional Reforms

1. **Preamble to a Civilizational Constitution.** Amend the constitutional preamble to recognize Nepal as a plural *Civilization-State* rooted in Hindu, Buddhist, Shaiva, Kirat Mundhum, Bon, animist, and Mithila philosophical traditions, while guaranteeing equal rights to all religious and cultural communities. Secularism should be interpreted as *sarva dharma samabhava*—equal respect for all faiths—rather than as state neutrality or hostility toward tradition.

2. **Middle-Way Federalism.** Review provincial boundaries and competencies to ensure they foster multi-ethnic cooperation rather than ethnic encapsulation. Establish inter-provincial councils and a National Civilizational Heritage Commission.

3. **Truth and Reconciliation through Restorative Philosophy.** Design transitional justice mechanisms informed by *satya*, *ahimsa*, Shaiva recognition of shared divinity, and Kirat/Bon ritual restoration. Victim-centered dialogue should be accompanied by community rituals of purification and renewal.

4. **Evidence-Based and Dialogical Governance.** Require policy deliberation to meet *Nyaya*-style standards of evidence and logical consistency. Establish permanent public philosophy forums at national and provincial levels.

### 4.2 Educational Transformation

1. **Civilizational Studies Curriculum.** Integrate comparative Nepalese philosophy—Shaiva Agamas, Upanishads, Ashtavakra Gita, Buddhist *Madhyamaka*, Kirat Mundhum, Bon cosmology, Confucianism, and Daoism—into school and university curricula. The goal is not religious indoctrination but philosophical literacy and ethical citizenship.

2. **Teacher Formation in Indigenous Philosophy.** Train teachers in dialogical and dialectical methods inspired by *Madhyamaka* and Confucian pedagogy, enabling students to hold multiple perspectives without falling into relativism or dogmatism.

3. **Oral History and Multilingual Archives.** Support community-based documentation of Mundhum, Bon, and shamanic traditions in native languages. Establish university research centers for Himalayan civilizational studies.

### 4.3 Community Reconciliation and Ecological Stewardship

1. **Interfaith and Inter-Ethnic Civilizational Councils.** Create permanent councils at district and municipal levels bringing together Shaiva priests, Buddhist lamas, Kirat *phedangmas*, Bon practitioners, Christian pastors, and Muslim imams for dialogue and conflict mediation.

2. **Revival of Customary Institutions.** Legally recognize *guthis*, Kirat councils, *kipat* land stewardship, and local mediation bodies as legitimate partners in governance, provided they respect constitutional rights.

3. **Sacred Geography and Eco-Cultural Conservation.** Protect mountains, rivers, forests, and groves identified as sacred by Hindu, Buddhist, Bon, Kirat, and animist communities. Frame conservation as a civilizational duty rather than as a technocratic project.

4. **Youth Leadership and Inner Cultivation.** Establish residential programs in which youth study the *Ashtavakra Gita*, practice Yoga and Buddhist mindfulness, participate in Mundhum and Bon rituals, and engage in community service. Such programs can reduce radicalization and build emotional resilience.

## 5. Directions for Further Research

This article opens several avenues for empirical and theoretical investigation:

1. **Ethnography of Indigenous Dispute Resolution.** Field studies on how Kirat Mundhum, Bon, and shamanic practitioners currently mediate land, family, and inter-village conflicts in Nepal.

2. **Shaiva Tantra and Social Justice.** Historical and sociological research on how Shaiva temple culture in the Kathmandu Valley has functioned as an integrative or hierarchical force across caste and ethnic boundaries.

3. **Federalism and Sacred Geography.** Analysis of how provincial boundaries align—or conflict—with indigenous sacred territories and ritual networks.

4. **Janaka and Nepalese Political Imagination.** A reception-history of King Janak in Nepalese literature, education, and political rhetoric, exploring his potential as a symbol of philosopher-kingship.

5. **Comparative Civilization-State Studies.** Comparative analysis of Nepal with other civilization-states (India, China, Russia, Iran) to identify distinctive features of Himalayan civilizational pluralism.

6. **Curriculum Impact Assessment.** Evaluation of pilot programs in Nepalese civilizational studies to measure effects on intergroup attitudes, civic engagement, and conflict-resolution skills among students.

## 6. Conclusion

Modern Nepal’s societal disputes cannot be resolved by applying Western political templates designed for different civilizational conditions. Liberalism’s atomized individualism and Marxism’s antagonistic collectivism have both deepened divisions by ignoring the integrative, harmonic, and transcendent dimensions of Nepali culture. This article has argued that Nepal possesses an exceptionally rich philosophical archive—encompassing the *Astika* systems, the *Upanishads*, the *Ashtavakra Gita*, *Shaiva* and *Pashupata* traditions, Himalayan *Bon*, the Kirat *Mundhum*, Buddhist *Madhyamaka*, Confucianism, and Daoism—that offers a more adequate foundation for national reconciliation.

Through *Advaita* and Shaiva immanence, Nepal can transcend the reification of identity without dissolving legitimate diversity. Through *Pratītyasamutpāda*, *Mundhum*, and Bon cosmology, it can recognize the mutual constitution and sacred embeddedness of its communities. Through the Middle Way and Confucian harmony, it can design governance structures that balance unity and autonomy. Through Daoist *wu wei* and indigenous customary institutions, it can reduce state-society friction and honor local knowledge. Through King Janak’s royal yoga, it can envision leadership as Self-knowledge and service. And through Dugin’s Fourth Political Theory, critically adapted, Nepal can assert its civilizational sovereignty against globalist homogenization without falling into hegemony.

The restoration of national integrity is thus not a project of forced assimilation but of philosophical recovery. Nepal must look inward to its own vast intellectual, spiritual, and ecological inheritance to discover the conceptual resources for peace, order, and flourishing. In doing so, it can offer the world a model of civilizational resilience: a diverse yet united polity rooted in *Dharma*, harmony, sacred geography, and the recognition that all beings are ultimately one family.
