# Objective 3: Tongsam Tongshim — Creation, Balance, and the Triangular Relationship

## Data Collection Methods and Interview Protocol

**Research Objective:** Analyze the Kirat cosmological principle of **Tongsam Tongshim** as a philosophical framework for creation (cosmogenic processes and ontological emergence), balance (cosmic equilibrium and relational harmony), and the triangular relationship (triadic ontology between Nature, Ancestors, and Humans).

**Central Philosophical Question:** *How does Tongsam Tongshim maintain cosmic, social, and spiritual balance through the triadic relationship of Nature, Ancestors, and Humans?*

---

## Conceptual Framework

The triangular relationship encompasses three ontological poles:

1. **Nature** — mountains, forests, water, celestial bodies, earth spirits
2. **Ancestors** — deceased lineage members, spiritual guides, cosmic ancestors
3. **Humans** — living community, individual consciousness, collective agency

These three poles are not separate domains but mutually constitutive relational forces held in dynamic balance through the principle of **Tongshim** (balance/harmony), which itself originates from the cosmogenic act of **Tongsam** (creation/emergence).

---

## Multi-Method Data Collection Approach

This research employs a **descriptive-interpretive qualitative design** combining Hermeneutic Phenomenology, Textual Analysis, Structural Myth Analysis, Ritual Ethnography, and Semiotic Analysis. Each method is designed to illuminate a different dimension of Tongsam Tongshim and together provide triangulated, philosophically rigorous findings.

---

## Method 1: Phenomenological Interview Protocol

### Overview

Depth interviews with Kirat knowledge holders explore the lived understanding of Tongsam Tongshim. The protocol follows **Van Manen's phenomenological interview principles**: open-ended, exploratory, sensitive to the lifeworld of the participant, and philosophically attentive to language and narrative. Questions are organized in nine progressive phases, moving from cosmological foundations to contemporary reflection.

### Interview Preparation

**Pre-Interview Checklist:**
- Obtain free, prior, and informed consent in the participant's preferred language (Kirat Rai, Limbu, or Nepali)
- Explain research purpose, use of data, and right to withdraw
- Confirm permission for audio or video recording
- Provide a summary of the research in culturally accessible language
- Establish rapport through informal conversation before formal interview begins
- Allow participants to open with prayer, ritual gesture, or self-introduction according to their custom

### Phase 1: Cosmological Foundation and Creation Narrative

*Purpose: Establish the ontological ground of Tongsam (creation/emergence) and understand how creation functions as a philosophical category in Mundhum.*

**Opening Questions:**
1. What does "Tongsam" mean to you? How would you explain it in your own words to someone who has never heard it?
2. How does creation happen according to Mundhum — is it a single, completed act, or is it a continuous and ongoing process?
3. What role does balance play in creation? Why is balance (tongshim) necessary for the world to exist and continue?
4. Who or what is the creator in Kirat cosmological understanding? Is there a singular creator or multiple creative forces?
5. Is creation in Mundhum primarily a material event, a spiritual event, or both simultaneously?
6. How does the Kirat creation narrative differ from other traditions you may know — such as Hindu, Buddhist, or Christian accounts?
7. Is there a "before creation" in Mundhum? What existed before the world came into being?

**Probing Techniques:**
- "Can you tell me more about that?"
- "Is there a particular Mundhum verse or chant that describes this?"
- "How did you come to understand this — through teaching, ritual, or personal experience?"

---

### Phase 2: The Triangular Relationship — Nature

*Purpose: Explore the ontological relationship between humans and the natural world in Mundhum philosophy, including the question of nature's agency and intentionality.*

8. How do humans relate to nature according to Mundhum philosophy? Is this a relationship of dependence, partnership, or something else entirely?
9. Are mountains, forests, rivers, and the sky conscious entities — do they possess awareness, will, or agency in Kirat understanding?
10. What is the nature of communication or relationship between humans and natural entities such as trees, stones, water, or the wind?
11. Do you believe nature has a "voice" or intentionality — that it can express itself, warn, guide, or punish?
12. What responsibilities do humans have toward nature according to Mundhum? What duties are prescribed?
13. How does harming nature — through deforestation, pollution, or disrespect — affect the cosmic balance (tongshim)?
14. Are specific natural sites (mountains, caves, rivers) understood as sacred or as dwelling places of cosmological significance?
15. Can nature exist in balance without humans? Or do humans have an irreplaceable role in sustaining the natural order?

---

### Phase 3: The Triangular Relationship — Ancestors

*Purpose: Explore ancestor veneration, ontological continuity between the living and dead, and the role of ancestors in maintaining cosmic balance.*

16. Who are considered ancestors in Kirat understanding — only deceased relatives, or do ancestral categories extend to spiritual beings, mythological figures, or cosmic progenitors?
17. Are ancestors still alive in some ontological sense? Where do they exist after death, and in what form?
18. How do ancestors communicate with or influence the living — through dreams, ritual, nature signs, or illness?
19. What is the relationship between the living and ancestors in maintaining cosmic balance? Do they depend on each other?
20. Do ancestors have agency in the world of the living — can they intervene, protect, punish, or guide?
21. How do seasonal rituals like Udhauli (autumn) and Ubhauli (spring) connect the living community to the ancestral realm?
22. What happens when ancestral relationships are neglected or broken — what are the consequences for the living and for cosmic balance?
23. Is there a distinction in Mundhum between recently deceased ancestors and ancient or cosmic ancestors? Do they perform different roles?

---

### Phase 4: The Triangular Relationship — Humans

*Purpose: Explore the unique ontological position, agency, and responsibilities of living humans within the triadic structure.*

24. What is the unique role of humans in the triangle of nature-ancestors-humans? What can humans do that nature and ancestors cannot?
25. Do humans have special responsibilities in maintaining Tongsam Tongshim balance — responsibilities that neither nature nor ancestors can fulfill?
26. How do human moral and ethical actions affect cosmic balance? Can individual behavior disrupt or restore the larger order?
27. What happens when humans break their relationship with nature or with their ancestors? What form does this disruption take?
28. Is human consciousness or will the primary mechanism through which cosmic harmony is maintained or broken?
29. Is the individual human or the community (clan, village, tribe) the primary agent of balance in Mundhum thought?
30. Do children and young people have a role in maintaining Tongsam Tongshim, or is this primarily a responsibility of elders and ritual specialists?

---

### Phase 5: Integrated Triangular Balance — Tongshim

*Purpose: Explore how the three relational poles function together as an integrated system and what the experience of balance and imbalance looks like in practice.*

31. How do nature, ancestors, and humans work together to maintain balance — is this cooperation automatic, or does it require intentional effort?
32. What would happen if one point of the triangle was neglected or permanently broken — could balance be maintained with only two poles?
33. Can you give a specific example from Mundhum narratives or from contemporary life where this triangular balance is clearly visible or felt?
34. What does "tongshim" (balance/harmony) feel like or look like in practice? Is it experienced as a sensation, a social state, an ecological condition, or something else?
35. How is balance restored when it is disrupted — what are the processes, rituals, or actions required?
36. Is balance static (a fixed state to be maintained) or dynamic (a process of constant adjustment and renewal)? Can you explain this in your own words?

---

### Phase 6: Creation Myths and Cosmological Events

*Purpose: Engage with mythological-philosophical narratives that ground the triangular relationship in its cosmogenic origins.*

37. What is the creation story in Mundhum? How did the world and the three realms (sky, earth, underworld) come into being?
38. How did the relationship between nature, ancestors, and humans originate — were they always distinct, or did they emerge from a common source?
39. Are there mythological beings, deities, or cosmic figures that embody or personify the triangular principle?
40. What role do sacred sites — mountains, caves, sacred forests — play in maintaining or expressing Tongsam Tongshim?
41. Is there an eschatology (end-time cosmology) associated with the balance principle — what happens if tongshim is permanently broken?
42. Are there Mundhum narratives that describe a time when the triangle was disrupted and then restored? What caused the disruption and how was it healed?

---

### Phase 7: Ritual Enactment of Tongsam Tongshim

*Purpose: Understand how the philosophical principles of Tongsam Tongshim are embodied, performed, and transmitted through ceremonial practice.*

43. Which Kirat rituals specifically enact or reinforce Tongsam Tongshim? Can you describe the sequence and meaning of such a ritual?
44. In Sakela, Sili, or other sacred dances, what cosmological principles are being expressed? What does the movement communicate?
45. What do offerings (to nature, to ancestors, to the sky or earth) represent philosophically? What is their ontological function — are they gifts, exchanges, or something else?
46. How do seasonal festivals (Udhauli in autumn, Ubhauli in spring) embody the triangular relationship — can you trace each element of the festival back to the triangle?
47. What is the role of sacred plants, animals, or ritual objects in maintaining balance? Do they serve as bridges between the three poles?
48. How do rituals repair broken relationships in the triangle? Is there a specific sequence or logic to ritual restoration?
49. Who has the authority to perform rituals that address the triangular balance — only specialists like Nakchhong, or can any community member participate?

---

### Phase 8: Contemporary Disruption and Restoration

*Purpose: Assess how Tongsam Tongshim is understood in the context of modernity, environmental change, and cultural transformation.*

50. How has modernization, deforestation, or urbanization affected Tongsam Tongshim in your observation?
51. Can the ancestral principle survive meaningfully in urban or diaspora contexts where community ties to land and ritual have weakened?
52. How can Kirat communities maintain cosmic balance in the contemporary world while navigating modern pressures?
53. What wisdom from Tongsam Tongshim is still directly relevant today — what remains applicable without modification?
54. How might the principles of Tongsam Tongshim contribute to addressing ecological crises, social fragmentation, or spiritual alienation?
55. Have you witnessed a restoration of Tongsam Tongshim in recent times — a moment when the community deliberately renewed its relationship with nature or ancestors?

---

### Phase 9: Comparative and Philosophical Reflection

*Purpose: Invite participants to synthesize their understanding philosophically and to position Tongsam Tongshim in relation to other worldviews.*

56. How does Tongsam Tongshim compare to other cosmological philosophies you are familiar with — such as Hindu, Buddhist, or Daoist worldviews?
57. What makes Kirat triangular cosmology unique or distinctive? What does it offer that other cosmologies do not?
58. Is Tongsam Tongshim best understood as a philosophy, a religion, a worldview, an ecological ethic, or something else entirely?
59. What would you most want the world — especially those outside Kirat culture — to understand about Tongsam Tongshim?
60. If you were asked to explain Tongsam Tongshim in one sentence to someone who had never heard of it, what would you say?

**Closing Protocol:**
- Thank the participant and affirm the value of their contribution
- Ask if there is anything they wish to add or correct
- Ask if they know of others who should be interviewed
- Invite any final questions about the research
- Discuss next steps for sharing findings with the community

## Method 2: Textual Analysis and Thematic Mapping

### Purpose

Systematically analyze Mundhum texts — written, transcribed, and recorded — to identify the structural presence and philosophical articulation of the triangular relationship across diverse hymns, chants, and cosmological narratives.

### Procedure

**Step 1 — Corpus Collection**

Gather all available Mundhum sources:
- Written Mundhum texts: Rai Mundhum, Limbu Mundhum (Fedangma chants), Chamling Mundhum, Kulung Mundhum
- Scholarly translations and commentaries: works by Iman Singh Chemjong, Bairagi Kainla, Narad Muni Thulung, Dambar Krishna Shrestha
- Institutional archives: Nepal Pragya Pratisthan, Tribhuvan University Central Library, UNESCO intangible heritage documentation
- Oral recordings made during field interviews (transcribed and treated as primary text)
- Published ethnographic accounts referencing Mundhum cosmology

**Step 2 — Code Identification**

Identify lexical and conceptual markers across the corpus for three primary categories:

| Category | Key Terms and Markers |
|----------|-----------------------|
| **Creation (Tongsam)** | tongsam, samjel, ulimi (origin/emergence), sirim (arising), cosmogenic verbs, emergence imagery |
| **Balance (Tongshim)** | tongshim, samel (harmony), dhan (equilibrium), cyclical restoration language, reciprocity terms |
| **Triangular Relationship** | sky-earth-water clusters, living-dead-spirit triads, human-nature-ancestor references, relational prepositions and verbs |

**Step 3 — Thematic Mapping**

Apply **Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase thematic analysis**:

1. *Familiarization* — read and re-read corpus; note preliminary impressions
2. *Initial Coding* — generate open codes for every relevant passage
3. *Theme Search* — cluster codes into candidate themes: creation, balance, triangularity, disruption, restoration
4. *Theme Review* — verify themes against full corpus; refine and merge where necessary
5. *Theme Definition* — write clear philosophical definitions for each theme
6. *Reporting* — produce narrative thematic maps and interpretive summaries

Create **visual thematic diagrams** showing how Tongsam Tongshim appears across different Mundhum hymns, chants, and narrative genres.

**Step 4 — Interpretive Synthesis**

Apply **Ricoeur's narrative hermeneutics** — specifically the three-fold mimesis framework:
- *Prefiguration (Mimesis I)*: the pre-narrative understanding embedded in Kirat cultural practice
- *Configuration (Mimesis II)*: the narrative world constructed within Mundhum texts
- *Refiguration (Mimesis III)*: the new understanding produced when philosophical readers engage with the text

This method extracts philosophical meaning from mythological-ritual narratives without reducing them to allegory or dismissing their ontological claims.

## Method 3: Structural and Comparative Myth Analysis

### Purpose

Apply structural anthropological and comparative mythology frameworks to reveal the deep logical grammar of Kirat cosmological thought and position Tongsam Tongshim within a broader cross-cultural philosophical dialogue.

### Procedure

**Step 1 — Identify Binary Oppositions**

Using **Lévi-Strauss's structural analysis of myth**, identify the key binary oppositions organizing Kirat cosmological narratives:

| Opposition | Kirat Cosmological Expression |
|------------|-------------------------------|
| Sky ↔ Earth | Celestial ancestors vs. earthly spirits |
| Living ↔ Dead | Human community vs. ancestral realm |
| Human ↔ Nature | Cultural order vs. natural world |
| Order ↔ Chaos | Tongshim (balance) vs. cosmic rupture |
| Creation ↔ Dissolution | Tongsam (emergence) vs. dissolution |

**Step 2 — Map Mediation**

Analyze how Tongsam Tongshim functions as a **mediating structure** that resolves or holds these oppositions in productive tension rather than collapsing them. The triangular relationship (Nature-Ancestors-Humans) may itself be the mediating structure — a third term that prevents any binary from becoming absolute.

**Step 3 — Structural Deep-Logic**

Extract the underlying **logical grammar** of Kirat cosmological thought: the rules, relationships, and transformations that organize creation narratives, ritual sequences, and ethical prescriptions. Document this as a structural schema.

**Step 4 — Comparative Mapping**

Compare Tongsam Tongshim with other triangular cosmological frameworks:

| Cosmological System | Triangular Structure | Key Parallel with Tongsam Tongshim |
|--------------------|---------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Hindu Trimurti** | Brahma (creation) – Vishnu (sustenance) – Shiva (dissolution) | Functional triad governing cosmic cycle |
| **Daoist Cosmology** | Heaven (Tian) – Earth (Di) – Humanity (Ren) | Relational triad with human responsibility |
| **Hegelian Dialectic** | Thesis – Antithesis – Synthesis | Triadic movement toward resolution |
| **Pre-Socratic Cosmology** | Anaximander's apeiron, Heraclitean fire and logos | Balance as cosmic principle |
| **Whitehead's Process Philosophy** | Creativity – Actual Occasions – God | Relational becoming and ontological emergence |

The goal is not to reduce Tongsam Tongshim to any of these frameworks but to use comparison to **sharpen the distinctiveness** of the Kirat cosmological contribution.

## Method 4: Participant Observation and Ritual Ethnography

### Purpose

Provide embodied, contextual, and experiential documentation of Tongsam Tongshim as it is enacted, transmitted, and experienced in ritual practice — not merely described in language.

### Theoretical Grounding

Apply **Merleau-Ponty's embodied phenomenology**: understanding is not only cognitive but embedded in gesture, movement, spatial orientation, and sensory experience. Ritual enacts philosophical knowledge in ways that transcend verbal articulation.

Apply **Clifford Geertz's thick description**: record not only what happens in a ritual but the layers of meaning, social context, emotional tone, and cosmological significance that participants themselves attach to it.

### Key Rituals to Observe

| Ritual | Season/Timing | Primary Cosmological Focus |
|--------|--------------|---------------------------|
| **Udhauli Festival** | Autumn (Mangsir/November) | Harvest, ancestor veneration, cosmic gratitude |
| **Ubhauli Festival** | Spring (Baisakh/April-May) | Fertility, renewal, human-nature reciprocity |
| **Sakela Dance** | Both festivals | Cosmological expression through movement and music |
| **Sili or Chasok Tangnam** | Household and community | Ancestor worship and lineage connection |
| **Death and Funeral Rituals** | As they occur | Soul's journey and reintegration into ancestor realm |
| **Village or Household Ceremonies** | Seasonal and life-cycle | Offerings to sky, earth, and ancestral spirits |

### Documentation Protocol

**Field Notes Template:**

| Element | What to Record |
|---------|---------------|
| *Setting* | Physical location, spatial arrangement, cardinal orientation of altars and participants |
| *Participants* | Who is present; roles (priest, elder, family, community); gender and age distribution |
| *Sequence* | Chronological order of ritual actions; transitions between phases |
| *Objects* | Every ritual object used; its material, color, placement, and handling |
| *Speech* | Chants, prayers, invocations; note language, tone, rhythm |
| *Gesture and Movement* | Dance steps, hand positions, directional movements |
| *Emotion* | Visible emotional responses; communal atmosphere |
| *Anomalies* | Moments of improvisation, hesitation, correction, or unexpected events |
| *Researcher Reflection* | Personal observations, uncertainties, emerging interpretations |

**Additional Documentation:**
- Photography and video with ethical consent from participants and community leaders
- Interviews conducted immediately after rituals (within 24 hours) to capture fresh interpretive reflection
- Follow-up interviews with ritual specialists to clarify observed symbols and actions

## Method 5: Symbolic and Semiotic Analysis

### Purpose

Decode the philosophical meanings embedded in ritual objects, sacred sites, spatial arrangements, and temporal structures that constitute the material and symbolic world of Tongsam Tongshim.

### Theoretical Framework

Apply **Peirce's three-category semiotics**:
- *Icon* — signs that resemble what they signify (e.g., a triangular altar mirroring cosmic structure)
- *Index* — signs connected to what they signify by causation or contiguity (e.g., smoke rising as a path to the ancestors)
- *Symbol* — signs whose meaning is culturally established by convention (e.g., specific flowers for specific spirits)

Apply **Victor Turner's ritual symbolism**: identify dominant symbols (those that condense multiple meanings) and instrumental symbols (those that serve specific ritual functions), and analyze their capacity to communicate philosophical truths about the triangular relationship.

### Key Symbols to Analyze

**Sacred Sites:**
- Mountains (Kanyam, Pathibhara, and locally significant peaks) as meeting points of sky and earth
- Caves as thresholds between the living world and the ancestral realm
- Springs and rivers as boundary zones between human and natural agency
- Sacred groves (forest shrines) as dwelling places of nature spirits

**Cosmological Markers:**
- Directional orientations in ritual space (East, West, North, South, Center) and their cosmological meanings
- Vertical axes (sky-earth-underworld) and their corresponding relational poles (ancestors-humans-nature spirits)
- Threshold spaces: doorways, crossroads, riverbanks — zones of ontological transition

**Ritual Objects:**
- Sacred thread (its color, material, and method of tying as semiotic text)
- Rice (abundance, life-force, offering to ancestors and nature)
- Alcohol (fermented grain as ritual substance bridging human, ancestral, and natural worlds)
- Flowers and plants with specific cosmological associations
- Animal sacrifices and their philosophical significance

**Spatial and Temporal Arrangements:**
- Altar orientation and the cosmological logic of placement
- Seating positions of ritual specialists relative to community members
- The lunar calendar structuring ritual timing and its connection to balance cycles

## Method 6: Genealogical and Cosmological Mapping (Visual Methodology)

### Purpose

Create visual representations of the triangular relationship to render Tongsam Tongshim accessible for analysis, comparison, and philosophical communication.

### Outputs

**1. Cosmological Diagram**
A visual representation of the Nature-Ancestors-Humans triangle, showing:
- The three poles and their internal complexity
- The arrows and flows of relationship (reciprocity, obligation, communication)
- The central principle of Tongshim (balance) as the relational field
- The origin point of Tongsam (creation) as the generative source

**2. Genealogical Chart**
Mapping living human lineage in relation to the ancestral realm:
- Living community → recently deceased → distant ancestors → cosmic progenitors
- Points of ritual connection and ontological continuity

**3. Seasonal Balance Calendar**
Visual representation of how Tongsam Tongshim operates across the annual cycle:
- Autumn (Udhauli): gratitude, descent, ancestral connection, preparation for winter
- Spring (Ubhauli): renewal, ascent, fertility, human-nature engagement
- Other seasonal markers and their cosmological significance

**4. Sacred Geography Map**
A map (or descriptive inventory) of significant natural and sacred sites and their cosmological roles in maintaining the triangular balance.

## Ethical Considerations

1. **Informed Consent** — Provide research information in the participant's preferred language (Kirat Rai, Limbu, or Nepali); obtain written or witnessed oral consent before recording begins.

2. **Cultural Respect and Sacred Knowledge** — Acknowledge that Tongsam Tongshim is living sacred knowledge, not merely historical or folkloric material. Seek explicit community permission before publishing specific ritual details, chants, or sacred site descriptions.

3. **Reciprocity and Benefit Sharing** — Explain clearly how the research will contribute to documenting and valorizing Kirat philosophy. Consider providing honoraria, educational materials, or community workshops as a form of reciprocal contribution.

4. **Community Engagement** — Involve community representatives in research design, interpretation of findings, and dissemination. Conduct a community presentation of findings before finalization.

5. **Data Protection** — Store all recordings, transcripts, and field notes securely with password protection. Offer participants the option of anonymity. Retain data only as long as necessary for the research.

6. **Positionality Statement** — Declare the researcher's epistemic positionality (insider or outsider to Kirat culture) and how this may shape interpretation. This is essential for maintaining research integrity within an indigenous philosophical context.

7. **Emic-Etic Balance** — Maintain active dialogue between emic categories (Kirat internal terms: tongsam, tongshim, sām) and etic categories (external philosophical terms: ontology, cosmology, triadic structure). Avoid imposing external frameworks that distort indigenous meaning.
