# Chapter 10: Major Schools of Buddhism

## The Diversity of Buddhist Traditions

Over the course of two and a half millennia, Buddhism has evolved into a remarkably diverse family of traditions, schools, and lineages. From its origins in northeastern India, the Dharma spread across Asia and eventually the entire world, adapting to different cultures, languages, and philosophical temperaments while maintaining its core insights.

Today, Buddhism is broadly divided into three major vehicles or traditions:

1. **Theravada** ("The Way of the Elders")
2. **Mahayana** ("The Great Vehicle")
3. **Vajrayana** ("The Diamond Vehicle")

Each of these encompasses multiple schools, lineages, and interpretive traditions, creating a rich tapestry of Buddhist thought and practice.

## Theravada Buddhism

### Overview

Theravada Buddhism claims to preserve the original teachings of the Buddha in their most authentic form. It is the dominant form of Buddhism in Sri Lanka, Myanmar (Burma), Thailand, Cambodia, and Laos, and it has a growing presence in the West.

The word *theravāda* means "the way" or "doctrine" (*vāda*) "of the elders" (*thera*) — referring to the senior monks who, according to tradition, preserved the Buddha's teachings at the First Buddhist Council shortly after his death.

### The Pali Canon

The scriptural foundation of Theravada is the Pali Canon (*Tipitaka*), the oldest complete collection of Buddhist scriptures. It is written in Pali, an ancient Indian language closely related to the language the Buddha likely spoke. The Tipitaka consists of three "baskets" (*piṭaka*):

1. **Vinaya Piṭaka (The Basket of Discipline):** Rules and guidelines for the monastic community
2. **Sutta Piṭaka (The Basket of Discourses):** The Buddha's teachings arranged in five collections (*nikāya*):
   - *Dīgha Nikāya* (Long Discourses)
   - *Majjhima Nikāya* (Middle-Length Discourses)
   - *Saṃyutta Nikāya* (Connected Discourses)
   - *Aṅguttara Nikāya* (Numerical Discourses)
   - *Khuddaka Nikāya* (Minor Collection — including the Dhammapada, Jataka tales, and other texts)
3. **Abhidhamma Piṭaka (The Basket of Higher Doctrine):** Systematic philosophical analysis of the teachings

### Key Characteristics of Theravada

- **Emphasis on the historical Buddha:** Theravada focuses primarily on the historical Siddhartha Gautama as the supreme teacher for this age
- **The Arahant ideal:** The goal is to become an arahant — one who has attained complete liberation through personal effort and insight
- **Monastic orientation:** While lay practice is valued, the monastic sangha is seen as the primary vehicle for the preservation and practice of the Dharma
- **Vipassana meditation:** Insight meditation, based on the *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta*, is the characteristic meditation practice
- **Abhidharma analysis:** Detailed analytical study of mental and physical phenomena
- **Conservative approach:** Emphasis on preserving the original teachings without addition or alteration

### The Commentarial Tradition

Theravada Buddhism has a rich commentarial tradition. The most important commentator is Buddhaghosa (5th century CE), whose monumental work *Visuddhimagga* (The Path of Purification) provides a comprehensive systematic presentation of Theravada doctrine and practice. This text organizes the entire path under the three trainings of morality (*sīla*), concentration (*samādhi*), and wisdom (*paññā*).

### Modern Theravada

In the modern era, Theravada Buddhism has experienced significant renewal through the Vipassana meditation movement, which has made insight meditation accessible to lay practitioners worldwide. Key figures include:

- **Mahasi Sayadaw** (1904-1982) — Myanmar; developed an influential noting technique for vipassana practice
- **S.N. Goenka** (1924-2013) — India/Myanmar lineage; founded a global network of vipassana meditation centers
- **Ajahn Chah** (1918-1992) — Thailand; founder of the Thai Forest tradition, which spread to the West
- **Bhikkhu Bodhi** (b. 1944) — American-born monk; prolific translator of the Pali Canon into English

## Mahayana Buddhism

### Overview

Mahayana Buddhism — the "Great Vehicle" — emerged as a distinct movement within Indian Buddhism around the 1st century BCE to the 1st century CE. It introduced new scriptures, new philosophical perspectives, and a new spiritual ideal: the bodhisattva, who aspires to achieve complete Buddhahood for the benefit of all sentient beings.

Mahayana became the dominant form of Buddhism in East Asia — China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam, and Taiwan — and has profoundly influenced Tibetan Buddhism as well.

### Key Mahayana Innovations

#### The Bodhisattva Ideal
While Theravada emphasizes the arahant who achieves personal liberation, Mahayana emphasizes the bodhisattva who postpones (or transforms) personal nirvana in order to help all beings achieve liberation. The bodhisattva is motivated by *bodhicitta* — the aspiration to achieve enlightenment for the benefit of all sentient beings.

#### New Scriptures
Mahayana introduced a vast body of new sutras, which it claims were taught by the Buddha but concealed until beings were ready to receive them. Important Mahayana sutras include:

- **Prajñāpāramitā Sūtras (Perfection of Wisdom):** Including the Heart Sutra and the Diamond Sutra; teach the doctrine of emptiness
- **Lotus Sūtra (Saddharmapuṇḍarīka):** Teaches the universal potential for Buddhahood and the skillful means of the Buddha
- **Vimalakīrti Nirdeśa:** Features a layperson who out-debates the Buddha's monastic disciples; challenges monastic elitism
- **Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra:** Emphasizes mind-only philosophy; influential in Zen Buddhism
- **Avataṃsaka Sūtra (Flower Garland):** Describes the mutual interpenetration of all phenomena; foundational for Huayan Buddhism

#### The Doctrine of Emptiness (Śūnyatā)
Mahayana philosophy generalized the early Buddhist teaching of non-self (*anattā*) into the universal doctrine of emptiness (*śūnyatā*). Not only persons but all phenomena are empty of inherent, independent existence. This is not nihilism but the recognition that all things exist only in dependence upon other things.

#### Buddha-Nature (Tathāgatagarbha)
Some Mahayana traditions teach that all sentient beings possess Buddha-nature — the inherent potential (or the inherent reality) of Buddhahood. This teaching emphasizes that enlightenment is not something to be acquired from outside but something to be discovered within.

### Major Mahayana Philosophical Schools

#### Madhyamaka (Middle Way School)

Founded by Nagarjuna (c. 150-250 CE), Madhyamaka is one of the two major philosophical schools of Mahayana Buddhism. Its central teaching is *śūnyatā* (emptiness) — the insight that all phenomena, without exception, are empty of inherent existence (*svabhāva*).

Nagarjuna's masterwork, the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way), uses rigorous dialectical analysis to demonstrate that no phenomenon has an intrinsic nature that makes it what it is independently of other things. Everything exists only in relation to other things — through causes and conditions, through contrast with other things, through conceptual designation.

Key Madhyamaka principles:
- All phenomena are empty of inherent existence
- Emptiness itself is empty — it is not an absolute or a substance
- Conventional truth and ultimate truth are two aspects of a single reality
- Emptiness and dependent origination are equivalent
- The Middle Way avoids both reification (treating things as more real than they are) and nihilism (denying reality altogether)

Later Madhyamaka divided into two sub-schools:
- **Svātantrika Madhyamaka** (associated with Bhāviveka): Uses independent logical arguments to establish emptiness
- **Prāsaṅgika Madhyamaka** (associated with Buddhapālita and Candrakīrti): Uses reductio ad absurdum arguments to demolish opponents' positions without asserting its own thesis

#### Yogacara (Mind-Only School)

Founded by the brothers Asanga and Vasubandhu (4th-5th century CE), Yogacara — also known as *Vijñānavāda* (Consciousness School) or *Cittamātra* (Mind-Only) — focuses on the nature and role of consciousness in constructing our experience of reality.

Key Yogacara teachings:

- **Mind-only (*vijñaptimātratā*):** What we experience as an external world is actually a manifestation of consciousness itself. This does not mean that the physical world is an illusion, but that our experience of it is inseparable from the consciousness that apprehends it.

- **Eight consciousnesses:** The five sense consciousnesses, mind-consciousness, afflicted mind-consciousness (*manas*), and store consciousness (*ālaya-vijñāna*).

- **Store consciousness (*ālaya-vijñāna*):** A foundational level of consciousness that stores the seeds (*bīja*) of all experiences, perceptions, and karmic imprints. These seeds are activated when appropriate conditions arise, producing the appearances that we take to be an external world.

- **Three natures (*trisvabhāva*):** The imagined nature (the way we mistakenly perceive reality), the dependent nature (the actual process of interdependent arising), and the perfected nature (reality as seen by an awakened mind).

### Major Mahayana Practice Traditions

#### Zen Buddhism (Chan)

Zen (*Ch'an* in Chinese, *Seon* in Korean, *Thiền* in Vietnamese) is perhaps the most widely known Mahayana tradition in the West. Originating in China through the meeting of Indian Buddhism and Chinese Daoism, Zen emphasizes:

- **Direct experience** over textual study or philosophical analysis
- **Meditation (zazen)** as the primary practice — "just sitting" with alert, open awareness
- **Koan practice** — working with paradoxical statements or questions designed to break through conceptual thinking
- **Transmission from master to student** — the direct, personal transmission of insight
- **Everyday life as practice** — finding the sacred in the ordinary

Key Zen lineages:
- **Rinzai Zen:** Emphasizes koan practice and the possibility of sudden awakening (*kenshō/satori*)
- **Sōtō Zen:** Emphasizes *shikantaza* ("just sitting") — the practice of open, choiceless awareness

Important Zen figures include Bodhidharma (the legendary founder), Huineng (6th Patriarch), Dōgen (founder of Japanese Sōtō), and Hakuin (reviver of Japanese Rinzai).

#### Pure Land Buddhism

Pure Land Buddhism is the most widely practiced form of Buddhism in East Asia. It centers on devotion to Amitābha (Amida) Buddha and the aspiration to be reborn in his Pure Land (*Sukhāvatī*) — a realm of ideal conditions for attaining enlightenment.

Key practices include:
- **Nianfo/Nembutsu:** The recitation of Amitābha's name — "Namo Amituofo" (Chinese) or "Namu Amida Butsu" (Japanese)
- **Visualization:** Contemplation of Amitābha and his Pure Land
- **Faith and devotion:** Trusting in Amitābha's compassionate vow to save all beings

Pure Land Buddhism emphasizes "other-power" (*tariki*) — relying on the grace and compassion of Amitābha — as complementary to "self-power" (*jiriki*) — one's own meditative effort. It has been particularly popular among lay practitioners because it does not require advanced meditation skills.

#### Tiantai / Tendai

Founded by Zhiyi (538-597 CE) in China, Tiantai (Tendai in Japan) is known for its comprehensive, synthetic approach to Buddhist doctrine. It classifies all of the Buddha's teachings into a hierarchical scheme, with the Lotus Sutra at the apex. Key concepts include:

- **Three Truths:** Emptiness, conventional existence, and the Middle Way — simultaneously present in all phenomena
- **Interpenetration:** All phenomena contain and reflect all other phenomena
- **Inclusive practice:** Combining meditation, study, devotion, and ethical practice

#### Huayan (Flower Garland)

Based on the Avataṃsaka Sūtra, Huayan Buddhism developed a remarkable philosophical vision of the mutual interpenetration and interdependence of all phenomena. Its key image is "Indra's Net" — a vast cosmic net with a jewel at every node, each jewel reflecting all the other jewels in an infinite play of mutual reflection. This vision expresses the profound interconnectedness of all things.

## Vajrayana Buddhism

### Overview

Vajrayana Buddhism — the "Diamond Vehicle" or "Thunderbolt Vehicle" — is a distinctive branch of Mahayana Buddhism that incorporates tantric practices as a means of accelerating the path to enlightenment. It is the dominant form of Buddhism in Tibet, Mongolia, Bhutan, and parts of Nepal, and has also influenced Japanese Buddhism (in the Shingon school).

The term *vajra* (literally "diamond" or "thunderbolt") symbolizes the indestructible nature of the awakened mind. Vajrayana claims to offer the fastest path to Buddhahood — not over many lifetimes but potentially within a single lifetime.

### Key Vajrayana Features

#### Tantric Practices
Vajrayana incorporates a wide range of tantric practices (*sādhana*) that use the body, speech, and mind as vehicles for transformation:

- **Mantra recitation:** The repetition of sacred syllables and phrases that are believed to embody the energy and qualities of enlightened beings
- **Visualization:** Detailed visualization of deities, mandalas (sacred diagrams), and subtle energy channels within the body
- **Mudra:** Ritual hand gestures that symbolize and invoke specific aspects of enlightenment
- **Ritual:** Elaborate ceremonial practices involving offerings, chanting, music, and dance

#### The Guru-Disciple Relationship
In Vajrayana, the relationship between the student and the guru (spiritual teacher, *lama* in Tibetan) is of paramount importance. The guru is seen as the living embodiment of the Buddha's wisdom and compassion, and as the essential guide for navigating the powerful practices of tantra. Devotion to the guru (*guru-yoga*) is considered one of the most important practices.

#### Deity Yoga
A distinctive practice of Vajrayana is deity yoga — the visualization of oneself as an enlightened being (a Buddha or bodhisattva), complete with their specific form, attributes, mantra, and mandala. The purpose is not worship of an external deity but the transformation of one's own ordinary perception of self and world into the pure perception of enlightened awareness.

#### Empowerment (Abhiṣeka/Wang)
Advanced tantric practices require formal empowerment or initiation from a qualified master. Empowerment transmits the authorization and blessings necessary to engage in specific practices, and it establishes a sacred bond between teacher and student.

### Tibetan Buddhist Schools

Tibetan Buddhism encompasses four major schools, each with its own lineage, teachings, and practice emphases:

#### 1. Nyingma (The Ancient School)
The oldest school of Tibetan Buddhism, tracing its origins to Padmasambhava (Guru Rinpoche), who brought Buddhism to Tibet in the 8th century. The Nyingma tradition is particularly known for:
- **Dzogchen (Great Perfection):** A direct, non-gradual approach to recognizing the nature of mind
- **Terma tradition:** The discovery of hidden teachings (*terma*) concealed by Padmasambhava for future generations

#### 2. Kagyu (The Oral Transmission School)
Founded by Marpa the Translator (1012-1097) and his famous student Milarepa (1052-1135). Key features:
- **Mahamudra (Great Seal):** The direct recognition of the nature of mind
- **The Six Yogas of Naropa:** Advanced tantric practices involving subtle body energies

#### 3. Sakya (The Grey Earth School)
Founded in 1073 and led by the Khön family lineage. Known for:
- **Lamdre (Path and Fruit):** A comprehensive system integrating sutra and tantra
- **Scholarly tradition:** Significant contributions to Buddhist philosophy and debate

#### 4. Gelug (The Way of Virtue)
Founded by Tsongkhapa (1357-1419), the most recent of the four schools. Known for:
- **Rigorous scholastic training:** Extensive study of philosophy, logic, and debate
- **Lamrim (Stages of the Path):** A systematic, graduated approach to Buddhist practice
- **The Dalai Lama lineage:** The Dalai Lamas are the spiritual heads of the Gelug school and have served as the political and spiritual leaders of Tibet

## Common Ground

Despite their many differences in emphasis, practice, and philosophical interpretation, all three major Buddhist traditions share:

- **The Four Noble Truths** as the foundational framework
- **The Noble Eightfold Path** as the way to liberation
- **The Three Marks of Existence** — impermanence, suffering, and non-self
- **The law of karma** and the principle of dependent origination
- **Refuge in the Three Jewels** — Buddha, Dharma, and Sangha
- **The commitment to non-harm** (*ahiṃsā*) and compassion
- **The ultimate goal of liberation** from suffering

These shared foundations make it possible to speak of "Buddhism" as a single tradition, despite its extraordinary diversity.
