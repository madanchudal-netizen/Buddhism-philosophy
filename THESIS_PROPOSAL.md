## 1. Introduction

Nepal stands at a unique crossroads of Hindu and Buddhist philosophical traditions, where centuries of co-existence have produced a rich tapestry of syncretic religious and ethical practice. Jhapa District in the eastern Terai region of Nepal exemplifies this confluence: its diverse population — including Brahmin-Chhetri, Limbu, Rai, Tamang, Newar, and Madhesi communities — negotiates Hindu and Buddhist identities in daily life, ritual practice, and ethical reasoning. This thesis proposes to investigate how two foundational philosophical concepts — **Dharma** (righteousness, moral law, cosmic order) and **Karuṇā** (compassion, empathetic action) — function as catalysts for social cohesion in a pluralistic society.

**Dharma** in Hinduism encompasses *ṛta* (cosmic order), *svadharma* (individual duty), and *sāmānya dharma* (universal ethics). In Buddhism, *Dhamma* signifies the teachings of the Buddha, the nature of reality, and the path to liberation. **Karuṇā** in Hinduism appears as *dayā* (mercy) and *ahiṃsā* (non-violence), while in Mahāyāna Buddhism it is elevated to a supreme virtue — the Bodhisattva's compassion for all sentient beings. This study investigates how the philosophical overlap and syncretism of these concepts promote shared ethical ground and community solidarity in Jhapa, Nepal.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 Theoretical Literature Review

#### 2.1.1 Dharma in Hindu Philosophy

The concept of Dharma is one of the most complex and multi-layered ideas in Indian philosophy. In the Ṛg Veda, *ṛta* denotes the cosmic moral order that sustains the universe (Holdrege, 2004). The Dharmasūtras and Dharmaśāstras — particularly the *Manusmṛti* — codified dharma as a socio-moral duty encompassing *varṇāśrama dharma* (duties according to caste and stage of life), *sādhāraṇa dharma* (common duties such as truth, non-injury, and charity), and *rāja dharma* (duties of the king) (Olivelle, 2005). The *Bhagavad Gītā* presents a synthesis where Kṛṣṇa instructs Arjuna on *svadharma* as inseparable from cosmic and social harmony (Zaehner, 1969; Minor, 1982).

Hiltebeitel (2011) argues that dharma in the Mahābhārata operates not as a rigid code but as a contested, context-dependent ethical deliberation. Doniger (2009) traces how dharma evolved from Vedic ritualism to encompass social ethics. Matilal (2002) examines the moral dilemmas within the epic tradition, showing that dharma inherently involves ethical complexity and cannot be reduced to a single commandment. These works establish dharma as a dynamic concept adaptable to pluralistic social settings.

#### 2.1.2 Dharma (Dhamma) in Buddhist Philosophy

In Buddhism, *Dhamma* takes on a fundamentally different orientation: it refers to the Buddha's teachings, the truth of dependent origination (*pratītyasamutpāda*), and the Noble Eightfold Path (Gethin, 1998). The Pāli Canon treats *dhamma* as both the natural law of causation and the prescribed ethical path leading to *nibbāna* (Collins, 1998). Keown (2005) argues that Buddhist ethics are best understood as a form of virtue ethics, where moral development is integral to the path of liberation.

In Mahāyāna Buddhism, *dharma* extends to the *Bodhisattva* ideal, where compassion (*karuṇā*) and wisdom (*prajñā*) are inseparable (Williams, 2009). Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* deconstructs essentialist notions of dharma, arguing that all phenomena are *śūnya* (empty of inherent existence), which paradoxically grounds compassionate engagement with the world (Garfield, 1995). The Tibetan and Newar Buddhist traditions in Nepal further develop these themes through tantric and devotional practices (Gellner, 1992).

#### 2.1.3 Karuṇā (Compassion) in Hindu and Buddhist Thought

Karuṇā occupies a central position in both traditions, though its philosophical elaboration differs. In Hinduism, compassion is embedded within the broader framework of *ahiṃsā* (non-violence) and *dayā* (mercy). Chapple (1993) traces *ahiṃsā* from the Upaniṣads through Jain and Buddhist philosophy, showing it as a pan-Indian ethical norm. The *Yoga Sūtras* of Patañjali list *karuṇā* as one of the four *brahmavihāras* (sublime attitudes), and the *Bhagavad Gītā* extols compassion as a divine quality (*daivī sampad*) (Whicher, 1998).

In Buddhism, *karuṇā* is one of the four *brahmavihāras* (along with *mettā*, *muditā*, and *upekkhā*) in Theravāda teaching (Harvey, 2000). In Mahāyāna, *mahākaruṇā* (great compassion) defines the Bodhisattva path: Śāntideva's *Bodhicaryāvatāra* articulates compassion as the motivation for seeking enlightenment for the benefit of all beings (Crosby & Skilton, 1995). Makransky (2012) explores how Buddhist compassion practices can be adapted for contemporary social engagement, while Nhat Hanh (1998) demonstrates the application of *karuṇā* to modern social ethics through "Engaged Buddhism."

#### 2.1.4 Philosophical Syncretism

Syncretism, from the Greek *synkretismos*, refers to the blending or reconciliation of different philosophical, religious, or cultural traditions. Stewart and Shaw (1994) offer a critical framework for studying syncretism, arguing against pejorative uses of the term and for its analytical value in understanding cultural negotiation. Baird (1971) applies the concept to Hindu-Buddhist interactions in the Indian subcontinent, showing how shared concepts (such as dharma, karma, and compassion) served as bridges between traditions.

Gellner (1992) provides foundational analysis of Hindu-Buddhist syncretism among the Newar community of the Kathmandu Valley, demonstrating how both religions coexist through shared ritual practices, complementary cosmologies, and dual religious identities. Slusser (1982) traces the architectural and iconographic evidence of Hindu-Buddhist synthesis in Nepal. Ramble (2008) examines syncretic practices in Himalayan communities, showing that the boundary between Hindu and Buddhist identity is often fluid and context-dependent.

#### 2.1.5 Social Cohesion Theory

Social cohesion, as theorized by Durkheim (1893/1984), refers to the bonds that hold society together — mechanical solidarity in traditional societies based on shared beliefs, and organic solidarity in modern societies based on functional interdependence. Putnam (2000) advances the concept through social capital theory, distinguishing between bonding (within-group) and bridging (cross-group) social capital. Berger-Schmitt (2002) proposes a two-dimensional model of social cohesion: (1) reduction of inequalities and exclusion, and (2) strengthening of social connections and solidarity.

Habermas (1984) contributes the notion of communicative rationality, where social cohesion emerges through rational discourse and mutual understanding. Sen (2009) offers a capabilities-based framework that values both individual freedom and collective solidarity. In the Nepali context, Bhattachan (2012) analyzes how caste, ethnicity, and religious pluralism both challenge and sustain social cohesion in post-conflict Nepal.

### 2.2 Empirical Literature Review

#### 2.2.1 Hindu-Buddhist Syncretism in Nepal

Empirical studies of Hindu-Buddhist syncretism in Nepal provide crucial context for this research. Gellner (1992, 2001) conducted extensive ethnographic fieldwork among the Newar of Kathmandu Valley, documenting how individuals simultaneously identify as Hindu and Buddhist, participate in rituals of both traditions, and negotiate religious identity contextually. Locke (1985) studied the Buddhist *vihāra* system among the Newar, showing how monastic institutions preserved Buddhist identity while accommodating Hindu practices.

Owens (1989) examined the cult of the *kumārī* (living goddess) in Kathmandu as a site of Hindu-Buddhist convergence. Letizia (2012) studied the contemporary dynamics of religious identity in Nepal, particularly in the context of secularism declared in 2006, showing that communities in the Terai negotiate multiple religious identities. Whelpton (2005) provides a comprehensive historical account of how Hinduism and Buddhism have interacted in Nepal's socio-political landscape.

#### 2.2.2 Social Cohesion in Pluralistic Societies

Empirical research on social cohesion in religiously plural societies offers comparative insights. Varshney (2002) studied Hindu-Muslim relations in Indian cities, finding that inter-communal civic engagement (trade associations, shared festivals) significantly reduces communal violence. Langer et al. (2015) measured social cohesion in diverse societies using indicators of trust, participation, and shared identity. Schiefer and van der Noll (2017) conducted a systematic review of social cohesion research, identifying trust, shared values, belonging, and participation as core components.

In Nepal specifically, Lawoti and Hangen (2013) examined how ethnic and religious diversity has affected social cohesion in the post-Maoist insurgency period. Tamang (2009) analyzed the intersection of caste, ethnicity, and religion in shaping community solidarity in eastern Nepal, including areas adjacent to Jhapa. Bennett (2005) studied how gender, caste, and ethnicity interact in Nepali communities to either promote or undermine social cohesion.

#### 2.2.3 Dharma and Social Ethics in Practice

Empirical studies of dharma as a lived ethical concept are relevant to understanding its cohesive function. Davis (2010) studied how dharmaśāstra continues to influence social behavior in South Asian communities, arguing that dharmic ethics operate not through formal law but through community norms and expectations. Acharya (2015) examined the role of dharma in mediating social disputes in rural Nepal, showing that traditional ethical frameworks remain influential alongside modern legal systems.

In the Buddhist context, Queen and King (1996) documented the rise of Engaged Buddhism in Asia, where Buddhist ethical principles — particularly compassion — are applied to social, political, and environmental issues. Leve (2007) studied how Theravāda Buddhism has been revived in Nepal as both a religious identity and a social movement, particularly among Newar and Tharu communities who use Buddhist ethics to challenge caste-based hierarchy.

#### 2.2.4 Karuṇā and Social Action

Empirical research on compassion as a social force provides evidence for its cohesive potential. Condon and DeSteno (2011) experimentally demonstrated that meditation practices focused on compassion increase prosocial behavior. Leiberg et al. (2011) showed that compassion training enhances altruistic behavior even toward strangers. In the South Asian context, Arai (2015) studied how Buddhist women in the Himalayas use compassion-based practices to build community solidarity and address social suffering.

Kraft (1992) examined how Buddhist compassion translates into social action in various Asian contexts. In Nepal, LeVine and Gellner (2005) documented how Buddhist organizations in the Kathmandu Valley use karuṇā-based ethics to run educational, health, and social welfare programs that serve Hindu and Buddhist communities alike. Darlington (2012) studied Buddhist monks in Thailand who apply *karuṇā* to environmental conservation, demonstrating the practical social dimension of Buddhist compassion.

## 3. Research Gap

Based on the above literature review, the following critical research gaps are identified:

### Gap 1: Limited Philosophical Analysis of Dharma-Karuṇā Interrelation Across Traditions
While dharma and karuṇā have been studied extensively within their respective traditions (Hindu or Buddhist), there is a significant gap in **comparative philosophical analysis** that examines how these concepts function **syncretically** — that is, how they merge, interact, and mutually reinforce each other at the point of contact between Hinduism and Buddhism. Most studies treat them within a single tradition's framework.

### Gap 2: Absence of Social Cohesion as a Philosophical Category in Hindu-Buddhist Studies
Existing studies on Hindu-Buddhist syncretism (e.g., Gellner, 1992; Slusser, 1982) primarily focus on ritual, identity, and institutional dimensions. There is a notable absence of studies that **explicitly connect philosophical syncretism to social cohesion theory**. The link between shared philosophical concepts and community solidarity has not been rigorously examined through a philosophical lens.

### Gap 3: Jhapa District as an Unstudied Site
The vast majority of empirical studies on Hindu-Buddhist syncretism in Nepal focus on the **Kathmandu Valley** and the Newar community. Jhapa District — a diverse Terai region with significant Limbu, Rai, Tamang, Brahmin-Chhetri, and Madhesi populations — has been **largely overlooked** in studies of philosophical syncretism, despite being a site of significant Hindu-Buddhist co-existence.

### Gap 4: Lack of Grassroots Philosophical Inquiry
Existing philosophical studies are predominantly textual and exegetical. There is a gap in research that examines **how ordinary people in pluralistic communities understand and apply** concepts like dharma and karuṇā in their daily lives and social interactions. The philosophical study of lived syncretism at the community level is virtually absent.

### Gap 5: Insufficient Bridging of Eastern Philosophy with Social Science Frameworks
While social cohesion has been studied through sociological and political science frameworks (Durkheim, Putnam, Habermas), there is a gap in studies that use **Eastern philosophical categories** (dharma, karuṇā, ahiṃsā) as analytical tools for understanding social cohesion. This represents a missed opportunity for cross-disciplinary dialogue.

## 5. Statement of Problems

Nepal is a country of profound religious and philosophical diversity, yet it has experienced significant social fragmentation due to caste discrimination, ethnic marginalization, political conflict (the Maoist insurgency, 1996–2006), and the tensions of post-conflict transition. Jhapa District in the eastern Terai epitomizes this pluralism: Hindu and Buddhist communities co-exist alongside diverse ethnic groups (Limbu, Rai, Tamang, Newar, Brahmin-Chhetri, Madhesi), each with distinct yet overlapping philosophical and religious traditions.

Despite this rich co-existence, the **philosophical dimensions of social cohesion** in Jhapa have not been studied. Existing research on social cohesion in Nepal focuses on political, economic, and sociological factors, while studies of Hindu-Buddhist syncretism concentrate on the Kathmandu Valley and emphasize ritual and identity rather than philosophical content. The fundamental question of whether **shared philosophical concepts** — specifically dharma (righteous duty, cosmic order, moral law) and karuṇā (compassion, empathetic action) — serve as binding agents for social cohesion in a pluralistic community remains unanswered.

Furthermore, contemporary Nepal faces a crisis of values amid rapid modernization, urbanization, and globalization. Traditional philosophical frameworks of dharma and karuṇā are being challenged by market-driven individualism and political polarization. Whether these ancient concepts retain the capacity to **catalyze social cohesion** in a modern, diverse, and rapidly changing community like Jhapa is a question of both philosophical significance and practical urgency.

The central problem, therefore, is: **In what ways do the philosophical concepts of dharma and karuṇā, as they are understood and practiced syncretically across Hindu and Buddhist traditions in Jhapa, Nepal, function as catalysts for social cohesion, and what are the philosophical conditions and limitations of this function?**

## 6. Research Questions

### Primary Research Question
**How do the philosophical concepts of Dharma and Karuṇā, as understood and practiced syncretically within Hindu and Buddhist traditions in Jhapa, Nepal, function as catalysts for social cohesion?**

### Secondary Research Questions

1. **RQ1:** What are the key philosophical dimensions of Dharma in Hinduism and Buddhism that are commonly understood and practiced by communities in Jhapa, Nepal?

2. **RQ2:** How is Karuṇā (compassion) conceptualized and practiced across Hindu and Buddhist traditions in Jhapa, and to what extent do these conceptualizations converge?

3. **RQ3:** What forms of philosophical syncretism between Hindu and Buddhist understandings of Dharma and Karuṇā are observable in the social and ritual life of Jhapa communities?

4. **RQ4:** In what specific ways do syncretic understandings of Dharma and Karuṇā contribute to social cohesion — including inter-community trust, shared moral norms, cooperative behavior, and collective identity — in Jhapa?

5. **RQ5:** What are the philosophical tensions, contradictions, or limitations in the syncretic application of Dharma and Karuṇā to social cohesion in a pluralistic setting?

6. **RQ6:** How do contemporary factors — modernization, urbanization, globalization, and political change — affect the capacity of Dharma and Karuṇā to catalyze social cohesion in Jhapa?

## 8. Research Methodology

### 8.1 Research Philosophy (Paradigm)

This study adopts an **interpretive-hermeneutic paradigm**, consistent with the philosophical nature of the inquiry. The interpretive approach holds that reality is socially constructed and can be understood through the interpretation of meanings, beliefs, and practices (Creswell & Poth, 2018). The hermeneutic dimension — drawing on Gadamer's (1975) philosophical hermeneutics — emphasizes the interpretation of texts, traditions, and lived experience within their historical and cultural horizons.

This paradigm is appropriate because the study seeks to **understand meanings** (how people understand dharma and karuṇā), **interpret practices** (how philosophical syncretism manifests in social life), and **analyze concepts** (the philosophical structure of dharma, karuṇā, and their syncretic intersection).

### 8.2 Research Design

The study employs a **qualitative research design** combining:

1. **Philosophical Analysis:** Textual and conceptual analysis of primary philosophical sources (Vedas, Upaniṣads, Bhagavad Gītā, Dharmaśāstras, Pāli Canon, Mahāyāna sūtras, Śāntideva's Bodhicaryāvatāra, Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā) to establish the philosophical frameworks of dharma and karuṇā in both traditions.

2. **Phenomenological-Hermeneutic Inquiry:** Field-based qualitative inquiry to understand how dharma and karuṇā are experienced, interpreted, and practiced by Hindu and Buddhist communities in Jhapa.

3. **Comparative Philosophy:** Systematic comparison of Hindu and Buddhist conceptualizations to identify syncretic convergences and divergences.

### 8.3 Study Area

**Jhapa District**, Province No. 1, eastern Terai of Nepal. Jhapa is selected because:
- It has a **religiously and ethnically diverse** population including Hindu and Buddhist communities.
- Multiple ethnic groups (Limbu, Rai, Tamang, Newar, Brahmin-Chhetri, Madhesi, Tharu) co-exist and practice varying degrees of Hindu-Buddhist syncretism.
- It is **under-studied** in the literature on Hindu-Buddhist philosophical syncretism.
- It represents a **Terai/plains** context distinct from the hill and valley contexts that dominate existing research.

Specific sites within Jhapa will be selected purposively based on the presence of both Hindu temples and Buddhist monasteries/vihāras, shared festivals, and mixed-religion communities.

### 8.4 Population and Sampling

**Target Population:** Hindu and Buddhist religious leaders, community elders, philosophers, practitioners, and lay community members in Jhapa District.

**Sampling Strategy:** **Purposive sampling** (criterion-based and maximum variation sampling):

### 8.5 Data Collection Methods

#### 8.5.1 Philosophical Textual Analysis
- Systematic study of primary Hindu texts (*Ṛg Veda*, *Upaniṣads*, *Bhagavad Gītā*, *Manusmṛti*, *Yoga Sūtras*) and Buddhist texts (*Dhammapada*, *Sutta Piṭaka*, *Bodhicaryāvatāra*, *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*) focusing on dharma and karuṇā.
- Analysis of secondary philosophical commentaries and interpretive literature.
- Construction of a **comparative philosophical framework** mapping convergences and divergences.

#### 8.5.2 In-Depth Semi-Structured Interviews
- 49–62 interviews with participants across all categories.
- Interview guide organized around themes: (a) understanding of dharma, (b) understanding of karuṇā/dayā, (c) perception of Hindu-Buddhist commonalities, (d) role of shared values in community harmony, (e) experiences of inter-religious cooperation, (f) contemporary challenges.
- Interviews conducted in Nepali (and local languages as needed), audio-recorded with consent, and transcribed verbatim.

#### 8.5.3 Focus Group Discussions (FGDs)
- 4–6 FGDs with mixed groups of Hindu and Buddhist participants to explore shared understandings and areas of tension.
- Each FGD comprising 6–8 participants.

#### 8.5.4 Participant Observation
- Observation of shared festivals (e.g., *Dashain*, *Buddha Jayanti*, *Losar*, local *jātras*), temple/monastery visits, community gatherings, and conflict-resolution practices.
- Field notes on observable manifestations of syncretic dharmic and compassion-based ethics.

#### 8.5.5 Document Analysis
- Analysis of local publications, community guidelines, religious pamphlets, and organizational documents from temples, monasteries, and community organizations in Jhapa.

### 8.6 Data Analysis

#### 8.6.1 For Philosophical/Textual Data:
- **Hermeneutic analysis** following Gadamer's (1975) method of dialogical interpretation — reading texts within their historical horizons while bringing them into dialogue with the research questions.
- **Comparative philosophical analysis** to systematically identify convergent and divergent dimensions of dharma and karuṇā across traditions.

#### 8.6.2 For Empirical/Field Data:
- **Thematic analysis** following Braun and Clarke's (2006) six-phase model: familiarization, initial coding, theme generation, theme review, theme definition, and report writing.
- **Interpretive phenomenological analysis (IPA)** (Smith et al., 2009) to understand participants' lived experiences of dharma and karuṇā.
- Use of **NVivo** qualitative data analysis software for coding, categorization, and theme development.

### 8.7 Trustworthiness and Rigor

Following Lincoln and Guba (1985), the study ensures:
- **Credibility:** Prolonged engagement, triangulation (textual + interview + observation + FGD), member checking.
- **Transferability:** Thick description of context.
- **Dependability:** Audit trail, consistent procedures.
- **Confirmability:** Reflexive journaling, peer debriefing.

### 8.8 Ethical Considerations

- Institutional ethical clearance from the university.
- Informed consent from all participants.
- Confidentiality and anonymity of participants.
- Respect for religious beliefs and cultural sensitivities.
- Right to withdraw from the study at any time.
- Responsible representation of Hindu and Buddhist philosophical traditions.
- Conducting research within the provisions of Nepal's laws regarding religious and cultural sensitivity.

### 8.9 Limitations

- The study is limited to **Jhapa District** and cannot be generalized to all of Nepal.
- The philosophical scope is limited to **Hinduism and Buddhism**; other traditions (Islam, Christianity, indigenous animism) are not examined.
- As a qualitative study, findings are **interpretive** rather than statistically generalizable.
- Researcher positionality (as an MPhil student in Eastern Philosophy) may influence interpretation; reflexive practices will mitigate this.
