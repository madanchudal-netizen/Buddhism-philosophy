# Chapter 1: Introduction

## A Philosophical Analysis of Dukkha in Modernity: Examining the Relevance of Buddhist Dissatisfaction to the Contemporary Human Condition

---

## 1.1 Background of the Study

The concept of *dukkha* (Pāli: दुक्ख; Sanskrit: duḥkha) stands as one of the most philosophically profound and existentially consequential doctrines in the entire Buddhist intellectual tradition. Proclaimed by Siddhārtha Gautama in his first discourse at Isipatana (the *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta*, SN 56.11), dukkha constitutes the First Noble Truth (*dukkha ariya sacca*) — the foundational diagnostic upon which the entire edifice of Buddhist philosophy, ethics, and soteriology is constructed. Yet dukkha is far more than a statement about pain or suffering in any ordinary sense. As Walpola Rahula (1959) insisted, to translate dukkha simply as "suffering" is both misleading and philosophically impoverished; the term encompasses "imperfection, impermanence, emptiness, and insubstantiality" (p. 17). It is, in its deepest philosophical register, an ontological characterisation of the very nature of conditioned existence (*saṅkhāra*).

The contemporary world — characterised by unprecedented technological advancement, global interconnectedness, material abundance in much of the industrialised sphere, and the proliferation of choices and freedoms — would appear, on the surface, to represent a decisive departure from the conditions that gave rise to the Buddha's diagnosis twenty-five centuries ago. Yet modernity presents a paradox that demands philosophical scrutiny: despite extraordinary material progress, contemporary societies exhibit pervasive and deepening forms of dissatisfaction, existential anxiety, meaninglessness, and what Byung-Chul Han (2015) has termed the pathologies of the "achievement society." The World Health Organisation has declared depression a leading cause of disability worldwide; rates of anxiety, burnout, and what sociologists call "anomie" have reached levels that suggest something structurally embedded in the modern condition itself, rather than merely contingent misfortune.

This paradox — material abundance coexisting with deepening dissatisfaction — is not merely a sociological observation. It is a *philosophical* problem that calls for philosophical analysis. It is the central contention of this thesis that the Buddhist concept of dukkha, far from being an archaic or culturally bounded doctrine, provides a remarkably precise and philosophically rigorous framework for diagnosing the structural conditions of dissatisfaction in contemporary modernity.

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem

The modern world faces what may be described as a crisis of satisfaction. Despite the Enlightenment promise that reason, science, and material progress would liberate humanity from suffering, contemporary societies are marked by escalating rates of psychological distress, a pervasive sense of meaninglessness, and what Zygmunt Bauman (2000) has characterised as the radical instability and impermanence of "liquid modernity." The conditions that were supposed to eliminate suffering have, in significant respects, generated new and more insidious forms of it.

This global phenomenon takes on particular significance in the context of Nepal — a nation where Buddhist philosophical traditions have deep historical and cultural roots, yet where rapid modernisation, urbanisation, and the penetration of consumer and digital culture are transforming the lived experience of younger generations. Jhapa district, situated in the eastern Terai region of Nepal, represents a particularly instructive site for investigating this tension. As one of Nepal's most densely populated and rapidly urbanising districts, Jhapa has experienced significant economic development, migration, exposure to globalised consumer culture, and the expansion of digital connectivity — all while retaining a culturally diverse population with significant Buddhist heritage (including Kirat, Limbu, Tamang, and Newar communities with Buddhist philosophical traditions). Youth in Jhapa (aged 20–40 years) represent a demographic cohort that is uniquely positioned at the intersection of traditional Buddhist cultural contexts and the pressures of contemporary modernity — navigating questions of meaning, satisfaction, identity, and well-being in a rapidly transforming social landscape.

The problem this thesis addresses is therefore both philosophical and empirical:

1. **The diagnostic-philosophical problem:** Can the Buddhist philosophical analysis of dukkha — particularly the canonical three-fold typology of dukkha-dukkha, vipariṇāma-dukkha, and saṅkhāra-dukkha — serve as an adequate philosophical framework for understanding the structural conditions of dissatisfaction in contemporary modernity?

2. **The causal-analytic problem:** Can the Buddhist causal framework of *paṭiccasamuppāda* (dependent origination) and its analysis of *taṇhā* (craving) illuminate the mechanisms by which modern social, economic, and cultural structures perpetuate cycles of dissatisfaction?

3. **The empirical-experiential problem:** To what extent do youth in Jhapa (aged 20–40) actually experience the forms of dissatisfaction that Buddhist philosophy diagnoses, and how do they understand, articulate, and navigate these experiences in relation to both modern conditions and Buddhist philosophical/cultural categories?

These questions have not been systematically addressed in the existing literature. While scholars such as David Loy (1996, 2008, 2015) have made important contributions connecting Buddhist thought to modern societal critique, and while a growing body of work in "Buddhist modernism" (McMahan, 2008) has examined how Buddhist concepts have been reinterpreted in the modern era, there remains a significant gap: no sustained, systematic study has deployed the canonical Buddhist typology of dukkha as a diagnostic framework for the structural conditions of modernity **while simultaneously grounding this analysis in empirical investigation of how these conditions are experienced by an actual population**. Most contemporary engagement with dukkha in the modern context has been either **psychological** (reducing dukkha to a mental health category), **therapeutic** (focusing on mindfulness-based interventions), or **popular** (presenting Buddhism as a lifestyle or wellness practice). The distinctly *philosophical* dimension — dukkha as an ontological and phenomenological analysis of the nature of conditioned existence — has been insufficiently brought to bear on the question of modernity. Moreover, the existing scholarship has been overwhelmingly Western in its empirical context, with virtually no systematic mixed-method research examining the lived experience of dukkha among youth populations in historically Buddhist societies undergoing rapid modernisation, such as Nepal.

## 1.3 Thesis Statement

This thesis argues that the Buddhist philosophical concept of dukkha, understood in its full canonical depth as encompassing three dimensions — *dukkha-dukkha* (the suffering of suffering), *vipariṇāma-dukkha* (the suffering inherent in impermanence and change), and *saṅkhāra-dukkha* (the suffering inherent in the very nature of conditioned existence) — provides a philosophically rigorous and diagnostically powerful framework for analysing the structural conditions of dissatisfaction in contemporary modernity, and that this analytical power is confirmed when the framework is empirically grounded in the lived experience of youth (aged 20–40) in Jhapa, Nepal. Further, it argues that the Buddhist causal analysis through *paṭiccasamuppāda* (dependent origination) and *taṇhā* (craving) illuminates the mechanisms by which modern institutional structures — particularly consumerism, digital culture, and the achievement-performance paradigm — perpetuate and intensify cycles of dissatisfaction in ways that are structurally analogous to the processes described in classical Buddhist philosophy.

Employing a **mixed-method research design** that integrates philosophical analysis with both quantitative survey data and qualitative in-depth inquiry, the thesis demonstrates that the forms of dissatisfaction experienced by youth in Jhapa correspond to the typological categories of the Buddhist dukkha analysis in ways that are both statistically measurable and experientially rich. The thesis does not claim that the Buddha anticipated modernity, nor that Buddhist philosophy offers a complete social theory. Rather, it demonstrates that the *philosophical categories* developed within the Buddhist tradition possess an analytical precision and diagnostic depth that makes them genuinely illuminating when applied to contemporary phenomena — and that this illumination is strengthened, not diminished, when subjected to empirical investigation among a population that inhabits both Buddhist cultural heritage and the conditions of contemporary modernity.

## 1.4 Research Objectives

The primary objectives of this research are:

1. **To elucidate the philosophical concept of dukkha** in its canonical depth, drawing on the Pāli Canon (particularly the *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta*, SN 56.11), the Abhidhamma, the *Visuddhimagga* of Buddhaghosa, and the Mādhyamika philosophy of Nāgārjuna, with particular attention to the three-fold typology and its philosophical implications.

2. **To assess the prevalence and patterns of dissatisfaction among youth (aged 20–40) in Jhapa** through a quantitative survey measuring life satisfaction, existential anxiety, perceived impermanence, consumerist orientation, digital dependency, achievement pressure, and related indicators — operationalised through validated psychometric instruments and Buddhist-philosophical categories.

3. **To explore the lived experience of dukkha among youth in Jhapa** through qualitative in-depth interviews and focus group discussions, examining how young people understand, articulate, and navigate dissatisfaction, impermanence, craving, and meaning-seeking in the context of their daily lives, cultural heritage, and engagement with modernity.

4. **To systematically apply the three-fold typology of dukkha** (dukkha-dukkha, vipariṇāma-dukkha, saṅkhāra-dukkha) as a diagnostic framework to the empirically documented conditions of dissatisfaction among Jhapa youth, demonstrating the analytical adequacy and illuminating power of Buddhist philosophical categories when brought into dialogue with lived experience.

5. **To analyse the causal mechanisms of modern dissatisfaction** through the Buddhist philosophical framework of *paṭiccasamuppāda* and *taṇhā*, mapping the three forms of craving (*kāma-taṇhā*, *bhava-taṇhā*, *vibhava-taṇhā*) to specific features of contemporary consumer, digital, and achievement culture as experienced by youth in Jhapa.

6. **To evaluate the philosophical and practical implications** of this mixed-method analysis for Buddhist philosophy (its continuing relevance and empirical groundedness), for the philosophy of modernity (what Buddhist categories reveal about contemporary dissatisfaction), and for youth well-being and policy in the Nepali context.

## 1.5 Research Questions

The thesis is guided by the following research questions:

**Primary Research Question:**
> How can the Buddhist philosophical concept of dukkha, investigated through a mixed-method approach among youth (aged 20–40) in Jhapa, serve as a diagnostic framework for understanding the structural conditions of dissatisfaction in contemporary modernity?

**Secondary Research Questions:**

1. What are the philosophical dimensions and typological distinctions within the canonical Buddhist analysis of dukkha, and how do they extend beyond the reductive translation of "suffering"?

2. What is the prevalence and nature of dissatisfaction, existential anxiety, perceived impermanence, and craving-related behaviours among youth (aged 20–40) in Jhapa, as measured through quantitative survey instruments?

3. How do youth in Jhapa understand, experience, and articulate forms of dissatisfaction, impermanence, and craving in their daily lives, and to what extent do their narratives resonate with Buddhist philosophical categories?

4. How does each dimension of the three-fold typology of dukkha (dukkha-dukkha, vipariṇāma-dukkha, saṅkhāra-dukkha) map onto the specific conditions of dissatisfaction documented among youth in Jhapa?

5. How do the Buddhist concepts of *taṇhā* (craving) and *paṭiccasamuppāda* (dependent origination) illuminate the causal mechanisms by which modern institutions — consumerism, digital culture, achievement pressure — perpetuate cycles of dissatisfaction among Jhapa youth?

6. What are the philosophical and practical implications of this cross-traditional, empirically grounded analysis for Buddhist philosophy, the philosophy of modernity, and youth well-being in Nepal?

## 1.6 Significance of the Study

This research contributes to scholarship in several important ways:

### 1.6.1 Contribution to Buddhist Philosophy
The study demonstrates the continuing philosophical vitality and relevance of classical Buddhist concepts by showing that dukkha, when understood in its full canonical depth, is not merely a historical or soteriological doctrine but a living philosophical framework capable of illuminating contemporary phenomena. By grounding this demonstration in empirical fieldwork among youth in Jhapa, the study moves beyond purely textual or speculative argumentation and shows that Buddhist philosophical categories have diagnostic power that is confirmed by the lived experience of actual populations. This counters the tendency — criticised by Žižek (2003) among others — to reduce Buddhism to either an exotic relic or a therapeutic technique.

### 1.6.2 Contribution to the Philosophy of Modernity
By bringing Buddhist philosophical categories to bear on the diagnosis of modernity, the study offers analytical tools that complement and, in certain respects, surpass the diagnostic capacity of Western critical theory. In particular, the concept of *saṅkhāra-dukkha* — the dissatisfaction inherent in the very nature of conditioned existence — provides a depth of ontological analysis that has no precise equivalent in Western social philosophy, which tends to locate the source of dissatisfaction in specific institutional arrangements rather than in the structural features of conditioned existence itself.

### 1.6.3 Contribution to Comparative Philosophy
The study models a genuinely *comparative* philosophical methodology — one that does not merely juxtapose Eastern and Western concepts but deploys Buddhist categories as active analytical tools for examining phenomena typically analysed through Western frameworks alone. This advances the field of comparative philosophy beyond mere description toward genuine philosophical engagement.

### 1.6.4 Contribution to the Field of Eastern Philosophy
For the field of Eastern Philosophy specifically, this research demonstrates that classical Buddhist philosophical categories are not museum pieces to be studied from a historical distance but rigorous intellectual tools that can be brought into dialogue with contemporary philosophical problems — a contribution that strengthens the case for the continuing academic and intellectual significance of Eastern philosophical traditions.

### 1.6.5 Empirical and Methodological Contribution
The mixed-method design itself constitutes a methodological contribution to the field. By integrating philosophical analysis with quantitative and qualitative empirical research among youth in a specific geographical and cultural context (Jhapa, Nepal), the study demonstrates that Buddhist philosophical concepts can be operationalised, measured, and explored empirically without losing their philosophical depth. This provides a model for future research that bridges the gap between Buddhist philosophical scholarship and social science research. The study also contributes empirical data on youth dissatisfaction, well-being, and meaning-making in a Nepali context — an area where existing research is limited.

### 1.6.6 Practical Significance for Youth and Policy in Nepal
The findings of this research have practical relevance for understanding and addressing the well-being of youth in Jhapa and similar rapidly modernising regions of Nepal. By identifying the specific forms and mechanisms of dissatisfaction experienced by young people, and by framing these through both philosophical and empirical lenses, the study provides a foundation for culturally informed interventions, educational programmes, and policy considerations that take seriously both the modernising aspirations and the philosophical-cultural heritage of Nepali youth.

## 1.7 Scope and Delimitations

### 1.7.1 Scope
This thesis focuses on:

- **The Theravāda and Mādhyamika philosophical traditions** as the primary sources for the analysis of dukkha. The Theravāda tradition (Pāli Canon, Abhidhamma, Visuddhimagga) provides the foundational typology, while Nāgārjuna's Mādhyamika philosophy provides the critical-dialectical dimension (the relationship between dukkha, śūnyatā, and the critique of essentialist thinking).

- **Youth aged 20–40 years in Jhapa district, Nepal** as the target population for empirical investigation. This age cohort is selected because it represents the generation most directly positioned at the intersection of traditional Nepali-Buddhist cultural contexts and the forces of contemporary modernity (urbanisation, digital culture, consumer capitalism, achievement pressure, migration). Jhapa is selected as the study area because of its (a) rapid urbanisation and modernisation, (b) cultural diversity including communities with Buddhist heritage, (c) high youth population, and (d) exposure to both domestic development and cross-border influences from India and the broader globalised economy.

- **A mixed-method research design** that integrates three strands: (i) philosophical-textual analysis of Buddhist canonical sources, (ii) quantitative survey research measuring dissatisfaction indicators among Jhapa youth, and (iii) qualitative inquiry (in-depth interviews and focus group discussions) exploring the lived experience of dukkha in the modern context.

- **Contemporary modernity** as experienced in Jhapa — characterised by urbanisation, consumerism, digital connectivity, educational and employment pressures, and the erosion of traditional social structures — understood through both Western critical social philosophy (Bauman, Han, Rosa) and the local Nepali context.

### 1.7.2 Delimitations

- The thesis does **not** engage extensively with Yogācāra (Cittamātra) Buddhist philosophy, except where it is directly relevant to the phenomenological analysis of dukkha. This delimitation is necessitated by the scope of an MPhil thesis.

- The thesis does **not** address Buddhist meditation practice or mindfulness-based interventions except as objects of philosophical critique (particularly in relation to the "McMindfulness" phenomenon identified by Purser, 2019). The focus is on *philosophical and empirical* analysis, not therapeutic application.

- The thesis does **not** claim to provide a comprehensive Buddhist social theory or a Buddhist political philosophy. It is a *diagnostic* analysis — concerned with understanding and characterising the conditions of dissatisfaction, not with prescribing comprehensive social solutions, though practical implications are discussed.

- The thesis engages with **selected** modern critical theorists (Bauman, Han, Rosa, Loy) rather than attempting an exhaustive survey of all Western analyses of modernity. These thinkers are selected for the philosophical resonance of their analyses with Buddhist categories.

- The **geographical scope** is limited to Jhapa district. While the philosophical analysis has broader applicability, the empirical findings are contextualised within Jhapa and should be generalised to other regions with appropriate caution.

- The **age range** of 20–40 years excludes adolescents and older adults. This is a deliberate delimitation based on the rationale that this cohort is the most actively engaged with the tensions between traditional values and modern pressures, and is at a life stage where questions of meaning, satisfaction, career, relationships, and identity are most acute.

- The study does **not** conduct a clinical psychological assessment. While validated instruments are used for quantitative measurement, the study is a philosophical-empirical investigation, not a clinical or psychiatric evaluation.

## 1.8 Methodology

### 1.8.1 Research Approach: Mixed-Method Design

The methodology of this thesis employs a **mixed-method research design** (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018) that integrates Buddhist philosophical analysis with quantitative and qualitative empirical inquiry. This approach is best characterised as a **convergent parallel mixed-method design** (QUAN + QUAL + PHIL), in which philosophical analysis, quantitative survey research, and qualitative inquiry are conducted in parallel strands, with the findings integrated at the interpretation stage to produce a comprehensive, multi-layered understanding of dukkha in the context of contemporary modernity as experienced by youth in Jhapa.

The rationale for a mixed-method approach is threefold:

1. **Philosophical adequacy:** The Buddhist concept of dukkha operates simultaneously at ontological, phenomenological, and experiential levels. A purely philosophical analysis, while necessary, cannot fully demonstrate the diagnostic power of Buddhist categories without engagement with the actual experience of the populations to whom the diagnosis is applied. Conversely, a purely empirical study without philosophical grounding would reduce dukkha to a psychological variable, losing its philosophical depth.

2. **Complementarity:** Quantitative methods provide breadth — measuring the prevalence, patterns, and demographic distribution of dissatisfaction indicators across a representative sample. Qualitative methods provide depth — capturing the texture, meaning, and nuance of lived experience that numbers alone cannot convey. Philosophical analysis provides the interpretive framework — the conceptual categories through which both quantitative and qualitative data are understood.

3. **Triangulation:** The integration of three methodological strands enables triangulation (Denzin, 1978), allowing the researcher to corroborate, complement, and challenge findings from each strand against the others, strengthening the overall validity and richness of the analysis.

This methodology involves three interconnected strands:

#### Strand I: Philosophical-Textual Analysis (Canonical Exegesis)

The first strand involves rigorous philosophical analysis of the primary Buddhist canonical and commentarial sources on dukkha. This includes:

- **Primary Pāli Canon texts:** The *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta* (SN 56.11), relevant suttas from the *Saṃyutta Nikāya* (particularly the *Sacca-saṃyutta*, SN 56) and *Majjhima Nikāya*, and the *Mahāsatipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (DN 22).

- **Abhidhamma literature:** Particularly the *Dhammasaṅgaṇī* and *Vibhaṅga*, which provide the phenomenological micro-analysis of mental states relevant to dukkha.

- **Commentarial literature:** The *Visuddhimagga* of Buddhaghosa, which offers the most systematic Theravāda philosophical treatment of dukkha within the framework of dependent origination.

- **Mahāyāna philosophical texts:** Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (particularly chapters XXIV–XXV), analysed through Jay Garfield's (1994) translation and philosophical commentary, and Śāntideva's *Bodhicaryāvatāra* for the ethical dimensions of dukkha.

- **Western critical social philosophy:** Bauman (2000), Han (2015), Rosa (2013), and Loy (1996) for the characterisation of modernity that provides the context for the empirical investigation.

The aim of this textual-philosophical analysis is **systematic** — to extract the philosophical structure, typological distinctions, and analytical categories that the Buddhist tradition has developed for understanding dukkha, and to present them with sufficient philosophical precision that they can serve as both the conceptual framework for the empirical strands and the interpretive lens for the integrated analysis.

#### Strand II: Quantitative Research

The second strand involves quantitative survey research to measure the prevalence and patterns of dissatisfaction among youth in Jhapa.

**Study Area:** Jhapa district, Province No. 1, eastern Nepal. Jhapa is selected for its rapid urbanisation, cultural diversity (including communities with Buddhist heritage — Kirat, Limbu, Tamang, Newar), high youth population, and its position as a site where traditional and modern forces intersect.

**Target Population:** Youth aged 20–40 years residing in Jhapa district.

**Sample Size and Sampling Strategy:** A sample of approximately 385 respondents (calculated using Cochran's formula at 95% confidence level with 5% margin of error), drawn through **stratified random sampling**. Stratification variables include: (a) urban/semi-urban/rural residence within Jhapa, (b) gender, and (c) age sub-groups (20–25, 26–30, 31–35, 36–40) to ensure representation across key demographic categories.

**Data Collection Instrument:** A structured questionnaire comprising:

- **Section A: Demographic profile** — Age, gender, education, occupation, income, marital status, religious/cultural background, urban/rural residence.

- **Section B: Life satisfaction and dissatisfaction indicators** — Adapted from the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS; Diener et al., 1985) and the Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ; Steger et al., 2006), supplemented by items specifically developed to capture the three-fold typology of dukkha:
  - *Dukkha-dukkha items:* Direct measures of experienced suffering — physical health, mental distress (anxiety, depression indicators using PHQ-4; Kroenke et al., 2009), economic hardship, and social isolation.
  - *Vipariṇāma-dukkha items:* Measures of perceived impermanence and instability — job insecurity, relationship instability, identity flux, and dissatisfaction arising from the transience of pleasures and achievements.
  - *Saṅkhāra-dukkha items:* Measures of existential dissatisfaction — meaninglessness, the sense that "something is missing" despite material adequacy, dissatisfaction with the pace and structure of modern life, and the felt inadequacy of conditioned existence.

- **Section C: Craving and attachment indicators** — Items measuring consumerist orientation, digital dependency/screen time, social media comparison behaviours, achievement/performance pressure, and fear of missing out (FOMO), operationalising the three forms of *taṇhā*.

- **Section D: Buddhist cultural engagement** — Items measuring familiarity with Buddhist concepts, participation in Buddhist practices, and self-reported relevance of Buddhist philosophy to daily life.

**Pilot Testing:** The questionnaire will be pilot-tested with 30 respondents from the target population to assess reliability (Cronbach's alpha), comprehensibility, and cultural appropriateness. Items will be refined based on pilot results.

**Data Analysis:** Quantitative data will be analysed using descriptive statistics (frequencies, means, standard deviations), inferential statistics (cross-tabulations, chi-square tests, correlation analysis, ANOVA/t-tests for group comparisons), and, where appropriate, factor analysis to identify underlying dimensions of dissatisfaction. Analysis will be conducted using SPSS or equivalent statistical software.

#### Strand III: Qualitative Research

The third strand involves qualitative inquiry to explore the lived experience of dukkha among youth in Jhapa in depth.

**Participants:** A purposive sample of 20–25 participants drawn from the quantitative survey respondents, selected to represent diversity across gender, age, urban/rural residence, education level, and Buddhist cultural engagement. Participants will be selected using **maximum variation sampling** (Patton, 2015) to capture the widest range of perspectives.

**Data Collection Methods:**

- **Semi-structured in-depth interviews** (15–20 interviews, 45–90 minutes each): Guided by an interview protocol exploring: (a) sources of dissatisfaction and suffering in daily life; (b) experiences of impermanence and instability in relationships, work, and identity; (c) feelings of existential emptiness or "something missing"; (d) patterns of craving, desire, and consumption; (e) engagement with Buddhist ideas, practices, or cultural values; (f) strategies for coping with dissatisfaction. Questions will use accessible language while being philosophically informed — e.g., "Do you ever feel that even when things are going well, there is still something unsatisfying about life?" (probing saṅkhāra-dukkha) or "How do you feel when something you enjoy comes to an end?" (probing vipariṇāma-dukkha).

- **Focus group discussions** (3–4 groups of 6–8 participants each): Organised thematically around: (a) consumerism, social media, and satisfaction; (b) impermanence, change, and life transitions; (c) meaning, purpose, and existential questions. Focus groups will enable the exploration of shared cultural narratives and contested meanings around dissatisfaction and well-being.

**Data Analysis:** Qualitative data will be analysed using **thematic analysis** (Braun & Clarke, 2006) within a framework informed by Buddhist philosophical categories. The analysis will follow six phases: (1) familiarisation, (2) initial coding, (3) theme generation, (4) theme review, (5) theme definition and naming, (6) report writing. An initial coding framework will be derived from the three-fold typology of dukkha and the analysis of taṇhā, but the analysis will remain open to emergent themes not anticipated by the philosophical framework. NVivo or equivalent qualitative data analysis software will be used to manage and organise the data.

### 1.8.2 Integration of Strands

The three strands are integrated at two key points:

1. **At the design stage:** The philosophical analysis (Strand I) informs the design of both the quantitative instruments and the qualitative interview protocols, ensuring that empirical inquiry is philosophically grounded rather than ad hoc.

2. **At the interpretation stage:** Quantitative findings (prevalence, patterns, correlations) and qualitative findings (themes, narratives, meanings) are brought together and interpreted through the lens of the Buddhist philosophical framework. This integration follows Creswell and Plano Clark's (2018) model for convergent design, using a **joint display** approach in which quantitative data, qualitative themes, and philosophical categories are presented side by side to reveal convergences, divergences, and mutual illumination.

The result is a **multi-layered analysis** in which:
- Quantitative data shows *how prevalent* different forms of dissatisfaction are among Jhapa youth.
- Qualitative data shows *how these forms of dissatisfaction are experienced, understood, and narrated* by the youth themselves.
- Philosophical analysis shows *why* these forms of dissatisfaction arise and *what they reveal* about the nature of conditioned existence in modernity, deploying the Buddhist categories of dukkha, taṇhā, and paṭiccasamuppāda as diagnostic tools.

### 1.8.3 Methodological Principles

The methodology is guided by the following principles:

1. **Philosophical Rigour:** All Buddhist concepts are presented with precision and fidelity to the canonical and commentarial sources, avoiding the popularised or diluted versions that characterise much contemporary engagement with Buddhism.

2. **Empirical Integrity:** The quantitative and qualitative strands follow established standards of social science research, including appropriate sampling, validated instruments, systematic analysis, and transparent reporting.

3. **Hermeneutic Charity:** The Buddhist philosophical tradition is treated as a sophisticated intellectual tradition whose categories are taken seriously as potential bearers of philosophical truth, not merely as objects of historical or anthropological curiosity.

4. **Critical Engagement:** The analysis does not uncritically assume the adequacy of Buddhist categories but critically evaluates their diagnostic power, acknowledging limitations and areas where the analysis may be incomplete or require supplementation.

5. **Comparative Integrity:** The comparative dimension of the analysis maintains the integrity of both the Buddhist and Western philosophical traditions, avoiding the temptation to force artificial equivalences or to subordinate one tradition to the other.

6. **Cultural Sensitivity:** The empirical research is conducted with sensitivity to the cultural, linguistic, and social contexts of Jhapa youth. Instruments are translated into Nepali and back-translated to ensure accuracy. Interview protocols use culturally appropriate language. The researcher's positionality is acknowledged and reflected upon.

### 1.8.4 Ethical Considerations

The research will adhere to established ethical principles for research involving human participants:

- **Informed consent** will be obtained from all participants, with clear explanation of the study's purpose, procedures, voluntary nature, and confidentiality protections.
- **Confidentiality and anonymity** will be maintained for all participants. Pseudonyms will be used in qualitative reporting.
- **Right to withdraw** will be communicated to all participants, who may withdraw from the study at any time without consequence.
- **No harm** — the study does not involve experimental interventions or sensitive clinical assessments. However, given that questions touch on dissatisfaction and existential concerns, participants will be provided with information about available support services.
- **Ethical approval** will be obtained from the relevant institutional review board/ethics committee prior to data collection.

### 1.8.5 Note on Translations and Terminology

Throughout this thesis, Pāli terms are used for concepts drawn from the Theravāda tradition (e.g., *dukkha*, *taṇhā*, *paṭiccasamuppāda*, *nibbāna*), and Sanskrit terms are used for concepts drawn from the Mahāyāna tradition (e.g., *duḥkha*, *śūnyatā*, *pratītyasamutpāda*). Where a concept is discussed across both traditions, the Pāli form is used as the default, with the Sanskrit equivalent noted on first occurrence. English translations are provided in parentheses on first use and at key subsequent occurrences. Primary texts are cited from the Pali Text Society (PTS) editions where available, supplemented by Bhikkhu Bodhi's translations for the Saṃyutta Nikāya and Majjhima Nikāya, and by Jay Garfield's translation for Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*. For the empirical research, survey instruments are administered in Nepali; all translated items cited in the thesis are provided with both Nepali and English versions.

## 1.9 Literature Review Summary

A detailed critical review of the literature is presented in Chapter 2. Here, a brief overview of the key strands of existing scholarship is provided to situate the present study.

### 1.9.1 Classical Buddhist Philosophical Sources
The canonical analysis of dukkha begins with the *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta* (SN 56.11) and is developed systematically in the Abhidhamma, the *Visuddhimagga*, and the Mādhyamika philosophy of Nāgārjuna. These sources establish the philosophical foundations — the three-fold typology, the causal framework of dependent origination, and the analysis of craving — that this thesis deploys.

### 1.9.2 Modern Philosophical Interpretations
Key modern interpreters include Walpola Rahula (1959), who established the canonical modern exposition of dukkha; Mark Siderits (2007), who brought analytic philosophical rigour to Buddhist concepts; Bhikkhu Bodhi (1994), who provided precise philosophical mapping of the path to dukkha's cessation; and Peter Harvey (2013), who offered an authoritative systematic overview.

### 1.9.3 Buddhist Philosophy and Modernity
David Loy (1996, 2008, 2015) represents the most sustained scholarly effort to connect Buddhist analysis to modern societal critique. David McMahan (2008) has examined how Buddhist concepts have been transformed in the modern era. Ronald Purser (2019) has critically examined the commodification of Buddhist practice.

### 1.9.4 Western Philosophy of Modernity
The thesis engages with Bauman's "liquid modernity" (2000), Han's "burnout society" (2015), and Rosa's "social acceleration" (2013) as characterisations of the modern condition that resonate with Buddhist philosophical categories.

### 1.9.5 Comparative Philosophy
The comparative dimension draws on Morrison (1997) and Panaïoti (2012) on Buddhism and Nietzsche, Collins (1982) on selfhood, Thompson (2014) on phenomenology, and Flanagan (2011) on Buddhist naturalism.

### 1.9.6 Research Gap
The primary gap identified is the absence of a **systematic philosophical application** of the canonical Buddhist three-fold typology of dukkha as a diagnostic framework for the structural conditions of dissatisfaction in contemporary modernity. This thesis addresses that gap directly.

## 1.10 Definition of Key Terms

| Term (Pāli/Sanskrit) | Translation | Philosophical Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **Dukkha** (दुक्ख) | Suffering, dissatisfaction, unsatisfactoriness | The First Noble Truth; the ontological characterisation of conditioned existence as inherently unsatisfactory. Encompasses three dimensions: dukkha-dukkha, vipariṇāma-dukkha, and saṅkhāra-dukkha. |
| **Dukkha-dukkha** | Suffering of suffering | The first and most obvious dimension: physical and mental pain, illness, grief, lamentation — suffering that is immediately experienced as such. |
| **Vipariṇāma-dukkha** | Suffering of change | The second dimension: the dissatisfaction inherent in the impermanence of all conditioned phenomena, including pleasant experiences, which inevitably change and pass away. |
| **Saṅkhāra-dukkha** | Suffering of conditioned existence | The third and deepest dimension: the unsatisfactoriness inherent in the very nature of conditioned existence itself — the fact that all compounded phenomena (*saṅkhārā*) are by their nature impermanent, dependently arisen, and devoid of enduring selfhood. |
| **Taṇhā** (तण्हा) / **Tṛṣṇā** | Craving, thirst | The Second Noble Truth identifies taṇhā as the origination (*samudaya*) of dukkha. Three forms: *kāma-taṇhā* (craving for sensual pleasure), *bhava-taṇhā* (craving for existence/becoming), *vibhava-taṇhā* (craving for non-existence). |
| **Paṭiccasamuppāda** / **Pratītyasamutpāda** | Dependent origination | The twelve-link causal chain explaining how dukkha arises through the mutual conditioning of ignorance, formations, consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense bases, contact, feeling, craving, clinging, becoming, birth, and ageing-and-death. |
| **Anicca** (अनिच्च) / **Anitya** | Impermanence | One of the Three Marks of Existence; the universal characteristic of all conditioned phenomena that they are transient, unstable, and subject to arising and passing away. |
| **Anattā** (अनत्ता) / **Anātman** | Non-self | One of the Three Marks of Existence; the absence of a permanent, unchanging self or essence in any phenomenon, including persons. |
| **Saṅkhāra** (सङ्खार) / **Saṃskāra** | Formations, conditioned things | All compounded, conditioned phenomena — everything that arises through causes and conditions. In the context of saṅkhāra-dukkha, refers to the totality of conditioned existence. |
| **Nirodha** (निरोध) | Cessation | The Third Noble Truth: the cessation of dukkha through the cessation of taṇhā. In this thesis, examined philosophically as the possibility of a genuine response to modern dissatisfaction. |
| **Śūnyatā** (शून्यता) | Emptiness | Nāgārjuna's central concept: all phenomena are empty of inherent existence (*svabhāva*), existing only through dependent origination. The philosophical link between śūnyatā and dukkha is central to the Mādhyamika analysis. |
| **Modernity** | — | Used in this thesis to denote the contemporary condition of late-capitalist, technologically advanced, globalised societies, characterised by what Bauman calls "liquidity," Han calls "achievement culture," and Rosa calls "social acceleration." In the empirical context of this study, modernity is examined as experienced by youth in Jhapa, Nepal. |
| **Mixed-Method Research** | — | A research approach that integrates quantitative and qualitative methods of data collection and analysis within a single study, alongside philosophical analysis. This thesis employs a convergent parallel design (Creswell & Plano Clark, 2018). |
| **Youth (20–40 years)** | — | The target population of this study: individuals aged 20 to 40 years residing in Jhapa district, Nepal. This cohort is selected as the demographic most actively navigating the intersection of traditional cultural contexts and contemporary modern pressures. |

## 1.11 Organisation of the Thesis

The thesis is organised into ten chapters:

**Chapter 1: Introduction** (the present chapter) establishes the background, problem statement, thesis statement, research objectives and questions, significance, scope, methodology (mixed-method design), and key definitions.

**Chapter 2: Literature Review** provides a detailed critical review of the relevant scholarship across canonical Buddhist sources, modern philosophical interpretations of dukkha, Buddhist philosophy and modernity, Western philosophy of modernity, comparative philosophy, and empirical research on youth well-being in Nepal, culminating in the identification and justification of the research gap.

**Chapter 3: The Philosophy of Dukkha** presents a systematic philosophical analysis of dukkha drawing on the Pāli Canon, Abhidhamma, Visuddhimagga, and Nāgārjuna's Mādhyamika philosophy, with particular attention to the three-fold typology and its ontological, phenomenological, and ethical dimensions.

**Chapter 4: The Condition of Modernity** characterises the structural features of contemporary modernity that give rise to pervasive dissatisfaction, drawing on Bauman, Han, Rosa, and Loy, and contextualising these features within the specific conditions of Jhapa, Nepal.

**Chapter 5: Quantitative Findings — Patterns of Dissatisfaction Among Jhapa Youth** presents the results of the quantitative survey, reporting the prevalence and demographic patterns of dissatisfaction indicators operationalised through the three-fold dukkha typology and taṇhā framework.

**Chapter 6: Qualitative Findings — The Lived Experience of Dukkha Among Jhapa Youth** presents the thematic analysis of in-depth interviews and focus group discussions, reporting the themes, narratives, and meanings through which youth in Jhapa experience and articulate dissatisfaction, impermanence, craving, and the search for meaning.

**Chapter 7: Integrated Analysis — Dukkha as Diagnostic Framework** integrates the quantitative and qualitative findings with the Buddhist philosophical framework, systematically applying the three-fold typology of dukkha (dukkha-dukkha, vipariṇāma-dukkha, saṅkhāra-dukkha) to the empirically documented conditions, demonstrating the diagnostic power of Buddhist categories.

**Chapter 8: Taṇhā, Paṭiccasamuppāda, and the Mechanisms of Modern Craving** analyses the causal mechanisms of modern dissatisfaction through the Buddhist frameworks of dependent origination and craving, mapping the three forms of taṇhā to specific features of contemporary culture as experienced by Jhapa youth.

**Chapter 9: Discussion — Philosophical Implications and Practical Considerations** discusses the implications of the integrated findings for Buddhist philosophy (its continuing relevance), the philosophy of modernity (what Buddhist categories reveal), comparative philosophy (the value of cross-traditional empirical-philosophical inquiry), and practical considerations for youth well-being and policy in Nepal.

**Chapter 10: Conclusion** summarises the findings, evaluates the thesis statement, acknowledges limitations of the study, and suggests directions for further research.
