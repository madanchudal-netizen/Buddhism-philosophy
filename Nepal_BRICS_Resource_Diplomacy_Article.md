## Executive Summary

This article examines Nepal's strategic navigation of BRICS resource diplomacy and evaluates its impact on Nepal's national development trajectory between 2015 and 2025. Nepal, uniquely positioned between two BRICS giants — India and China — has increasingly engaged with the BRICS institutional ecosystem, including the New Development Bank (NDB) and the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), while simultaneously maintaining its traditional diplomatic balancing act. The paper argues that Nepal has evolved from a passive recipient of foreign aid into an active diplomatic agent leveraging competition among BRICS members — particularly in hydropower, water resources, and mineral sectors — to extract greater development concessions. Through the lens of resource diplomacy and small state strategic theory, this article demonstrates that Nepal's resource endowments, particularly its vast hydropower potential (~83,000 MW), function as geopolitical currency in its negotiations with BRICS members. Empirical analysis of flagship projects — including the Upper Tamakoshi Hydroelectric Project, Budhi Gandaki Hydropower Project, Arun-3 Hydropower Project, and BRI-linked connectivity projects — reveals a pattern of strategic utilization of inter-BRICS competition. However, the paper also identifies significant structural risks: asymmetric power relations, debt sustainability concerns, and implementation gaps. Policy recommendations are provided for Nepalese policymakers to maximise development gains while mitigating geopolitical vulnerabilities.

## Abstract

Nepal's position as a landlocked Himalayan state sandwiched between two BRICS members — India and China — provides a unique analytical lens through which to examine small state agency in resource diplomacy. This article investigates the central research question: **How has Nepal strategically navigated BRICS resource diplomacy to advance its national development objectives between 2015 and 2025?** Employing a qualitative case study methodology supplemented by secondary data from government reports, multilateral institutional documents, and academic literature, this study analyses Nepal's engagement across four dimensions: hydropower diplomacy, multilateral financing through the New Development Bank (NDB), Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) connectivity projects, and South-South cooperation frameworks. The findings reveal that Nepal has adopted a "competitive leveraging" strategy — deliberately cultivating competing BRICS interests in its hydropower sector and transit corridors to negotiate better project terms, financing conditions, and technology transfers. Specific case studies including the Arun-3 Hydropower Project (India, 900 MW), Upper Tamakoshi (Nepal domestic), and BRI-linked Kerung-Kathmandu railway demonstrate both the opportunities and structural limitations of this approach. The paper identifies a significant research gap in existing scholarship: while bilateral Nepal–India and Nepal–China relations are well-documented, the *multilateral* dimension of Nepal's BRICS engagement and Nepal's proactive agency therein remain virtually unexamined. This paper contributes to the nascent literature on small state resource diplomacy in Asia, extends BRICS scholarship beyond Africa-centric cases, and offers actionable policy frameworks for Nepalese development planners and Multinational Enterprises (MNEs) operating in the Himalayan resource sector. Theoretical contributions include a novel "Himalayan Balancing Model" applicable to other small, resource-rich states navigating great power competition.

**Keywords:** Nepal, BRICS, Resource Diplomacy, Hydropower, New Development Bank, Belt and Road Initiative, Small State Agency, Geopolitical Balancing, South Asian Development, Multinational Enterprises

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Nepal's Geostrategic Significance

Nepal occupies a singular position in contemporary Asian geopolitics. A landlocked nation of approximately 30 million people, Nepal is bordered to the north by the Tibet Autonomous Region of China and on three remaining sides by India — two of the most consequential emerging powers in the twenty-first century global order and both core members of the BRICS grouping (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa). This geographic reality, which has historically been experienced as a constraint, has increasingly been reframed in Nepalese foreign policy discourse as a strategic asset: the country sits at the nexus of two civilisational powers with deep historical interests in Himalayan resources, trade routes, and political influence (Muni, 2012; Upreti, 2016).

The period from 2015 to 2025 has been particularly transformative for Nepal's engagement with BRICS-aligned resource diplomacy. Following the devastating 2015 Gorkha earthquake, which catalysed an extraordinary mobilisation of international assistance including from BRICS members, Nepal's political leadership increasingly recognised the strategic value of its hydropower potential as both a development resource and a diplomatic instrument. With an estimated technically feasible hydropower capacity of 83,000 megawatts (MW) — of which less than 2,500 MW had been harnessed as of 2020 — Nepal possesses resource endowments that have attracted intense interest from Indian, Chinese, and other BRICS-affiliated investors (International Energy Agency, 2021; Nepal Electricity Authority, 2022).

### 1.2 The BRICS Framework and Small State Engagement

The BRICS grouping, while not a formal multilateral organisation comparable to the United Nations or World Trade Organisation, has evolved into a significant framework for South-South cooperation, alternative development financing, and geopolitical signalling (Stuenkel, 2020; Prashad, 2013). The establishment of the New Development Bank (NDB) in 2015 — headquartered in Shanghai with initial capitalisation of USD 50 billion — provided BRICS with a concrete institutional mechanism for financing infrastructure and development projects beyond the traditional Bretton Woods architecture. For Nepal, this development opened new pathways for development financing that were not conditioned by the stringent governance and structural adjustment requirements associated with World Bank or International Monetary Fund lending (Humphrey, 2015).

Simultaneously, China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), which Nepal formally joined in May 2017 through a Framework Agreement with China, introduced a new dimension to Nepal's resource diplomacy calculus. The BRI promised transformative connectivity infrastructure — most dramatically, a trans-Himalayan railway connecting Lhasa to Kathmandu — that could fundamentally alter Nepal's landlocked status and open new economic corridors (Wang, 2019; Huang, 2016). The geopolitical implications of this potential connectivity were not lost on India, Nepal's dominant trading partner and the source of approximately 65% of Nepal's imports, which responded with its own enhanced infrastructure commitments (Pant, 2017).

### 1.3 Significance of the Research

The interaction between Nepal's resource endowments and the competitive interests of BRICS members represents a microcosm of broader trends in twenty-first century development geopolitics. As BRICS continues to expand — admitting Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Ethiopia, Iran, Egypt, and Argentina in 2024 — the framework's influence on developing countries' strategic choices is growing. Nepal's experience offers instructive lessons for other small, resource-endowed states navigating multipolar great power competition.

From a Multinational Enterprises (MNE) perspective, Nepal's resource sector presents a case study of how geopolitical competition shapes investment environments, risk landscapes, and business opportunities for international corporations. State-owned enterprises (SOEs) from BRICS members — including India's SJVN Limited, NHPC Limited, and China's PowerChina, CGGC — operate as quasi-MNEs in Nepal's energy sector, blurring the conventional distinction between commercial investment and geopolitical statecraft (Leutert, 2019; Thacker, 2022).

This article is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews existing scholarship on BRICS, resource diplomacy, and South Asian geopolitics; Section 3 presents the conceptual framework; Section 4 provides the analytical core examining BRICS member engagement and Nepal's strategic responses; Section 5 identifies research contributions and remaining gaps; Section 6 concludes with policy implications.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 BRICS Scholarship: Evolution and Debates

The academic literature on BRICS has grown substantially since Jim O'Neill's original "BRIC" formulation in 2001 (O'Neill, 2001). Early scholarship focused predominantly on BRICS as an economic phenomenon — projecting the collective economic rise of Brazil, Russia, India, and China and their implications for the global balance of economic power (Wilson & Purushothaman, 2003; Armijo, 2007). Subsequent scholarship broadened to examine BRICS as a geopolitical project challenging Western hegemony in international institutions, trade regimes, and financial architectures (Stuenkel, 2020; Pant, 2013; Prashad, 2013).

A significant body of literature has examined BRICS engagement with the African continent, where the grouping's members — particularly China, India, and Brazil — have made substantial infrastructure investments tied to natural resource extraction (Carmody, 2011; Taylor, 2014; Alden, 2007). These Africa-focused studies have generated sophisticated theoretical frameworks for understanding how BRICS members pursue resource diplomacy through a combination of infrastructure financing, technical assistance, trade agreements, and political alignment. However, as Wang (2019) notes, these frameworks have been inadequately applied to South Asian contexts, where the geopolitical dynamics differ substantially due to the direct territorial adjacency of multiple BRICS members.

Stuenkel (2020) provides a comprehensive account of the BRICS institutional ecosystem, tracing the evolution from informal diplomatic coordination to the establishment of the NDB, the Contingent Reserve Arrangement (CRA), and the BRICS Business Council. Importantly, Stuenkel argues that BRICS coherence has been periodically undermined by bilateral tensions among members — most notably between India and China — which has constrained collective action but paradoxically created opportunities for smaller states to exploit inter-member competition.

### 2.2 Resource Diplomacy: Theoretical Foundations

The concept of resource diplomacy has been developed across international relations, development studies, and international political economy scholarship. Klare (2012) provides a foundational account of how competition for scarce natural resources — oil, water, minerals, and increasingly renewable energy sources — has become a structural driver of twenty-first century geopolitics. Ross (2012) and Humphreys, Sachs, and Stiglitz (2007) examine the "resource curse" hypothesis, documenting how resource wealth can paradoxically retard development when governance institutions are weak.

More relevant to the Nepal context is scholarship on hydropower diplomacy, a relatively nascent subfield. Mirumachi (2015) argues that transboundary water resources create complex interdependencies between upstream and downstream states, generating both conflict potential and cooperation incentives. Zeitoun and Warner (2006) develop a "hydro-hegemony" framework that identifies how powerful riparian states assert structural dominance over water resource allocation. Shrestha (2018) applies these frameworks to Nepal specifically, documenting how India's downstream position and its leverage over Nepalese energy exports has historically constrained Nepal's hydropower development options.

Chellaney (2011) provides a comprehensive account of China's strategic use of its upstream position on Asian river systems, arguing that China's dam-building programme on the Brahmaputra, Mekong, and Salween rivers constitutes a form of "water nationalism" that gives Beijing significant leverage over downstream neighbours. Nepal, though not directly a downstream state relative to China on major shared rivers, is nonetheless affected by these broader dynamics through China's interests in Himalayan watershed management and transboundary connectivity.

### 2.3 Small State Strategies in International Relations

The theoretical literature on small state strategies in international relations is relevant to understanding Nepal's diplomatic options. Keohane (1969) established the foundational distinction between "system-affecting" and "system-ineffectual" small states, while subsequent scholarship has documented the diverse strategies small states employ to maximise their autonomy and developmental gains in an anarchic international system (Hey, 2003; Thorhallsson, 2006).

Particularly relevant is the "balancing" literature. Walt (1987) distinguishes between "bandwagoning" — aligning with a dominant power — and "balancing" — building countervailing coalitions against a dominant power. Small states in asymmetric environments often face pressure to bandwagon but may prefer balancing strategies when multiple great powers compete for their alignment. Nepal's foreign policy between 2015 and 2025 can be read as an attempt to sustain a balancing posture, leveraging China's engagement to prevent excessive Indian dominance while maintaining Indian connectivity to prevent excessive Chinese dependence (Ogura, 2008; Thapa, 2018).

Wivel and Thorhallsson (2018) argue that small states increasingly exploit "niche diplomacy" — specialising in specific issue areas where they possess comparative advantages — to punch above their geopolitical weight. Nepal's hydropower resources constitute precisely such a niche: a sector in which Nepal possesses resource abundance that both India and China need, creating structural leverage that transcends Nepal's nominal power deficit.

### 2.4 BRICS-Nepal Studies: Existing Literature and Gaps

Direct academic scholarship on Nepal-BRICS relations is remarkably sparse. Muni (2012) and Baral (2012) provide comprehensive accounts of Nepal's foreign policy, including its engagement with India and China, but predate the NDB's establishment and Nepal's BRI accession. Upreti (2016) examines Nepal's post-earthquake reconstruction and the role of international actors including BRICS members, but from a humanitarian rather than resource diplomacy perspective.

More recent scholarship by Adhikari (2020) and Bhattarai (2021) examines specific bilateral dimensions — Adhikari on Nepal-China BRI engagement, Bhattarai on Nepal-India hydropower diplomacy — but neither frames these within a multilateral BRICS analytical framework. Shrestha (2018) comes closest to the current paper's concerns, examining Nepal's hydropower sector from a geopolitical perspective, but focuses primarily on the India-Nepal dyad.

The gap is clear: no existing study examines Nepal's *multilateral* engagement with the BRICS ecosystem as a whole, nor has any study systematically analysed Nepal's *strategic agency* in leveraging BRICS inter-member competition for development advantage. This paper addresses both lacunae.

## 3. Conceptual Framework

### 3.1 Resource Diplomacy as a Dual-Direction Instrument

This paper employs resource diplomacy as its primary analytical lens, but with an important theoretical modification. In conventional usage, "resource diplomacy" refers to the diplomatic strategies employed by resource-*importing* states to secure access to natural resources held by exporting states (Klare, 2012). However, this paper argues that resource diplomacy can be conceived as a dual-direction instrument: resource-*holding* states like Nepal can equally employ their resource endowments as diplomatic instruments to attract investment, negotiate favourable terms, and leverage competing interests.

This "supplier-side" resource diplomacy operates through three mechanisms:

1. **Competitive Attraction:** Deliberately cultivating competing interests from multiple resource-seeking actors to generate a competitive market for resource access rights.
2. **Conditionality Reciprocity:** Using resource access as a bargaining chip to extract concessions in financing terms, technology transfer, employment provisions, and political agreements.
3. **Strategic Sequencing:** Timing resource access negotiations to exploit moments of heightened competition between resource-seeking actors, maximising the price extracted.

Nepal's hydropower diplomacy with India and China demonstrates all three mechanisms, as detailed in the analysis section.

### 3.2 Small State Strategic Theory: The Himalayan Balancing Model

Building on existing small state theory, this paper proposes a novel "Himalayan Balancing Model" (HBM) as a conceptual framework for understanding Nepal's strategic behaviour. The HBM consists of four analytical components:

**Component 1 — Resource Niche Identification:** Nepal's recognition of its hydropower endowments as a strategic asset rather than merely a development resource. This shift in framing — from development object to diplomatic instrument — is foundational to Nepal's evolving strategy.

**Component 2 — Competitive Leveraging:** The deliberate cultivation of competing BRICS member interests in Nepal's resource sectors, creating a competitive dynamic that enhances Nepal's bargaining position.

**Component 3 — Multilateral Anchoring:** Nepal's strategic use of multilateral frameworks — the NDB, BRI, SAARC, and South-South cooperation — to constrain the dominance of any single bilateral partner and create institutional buffers against coercion.

**Component 4 — Incremental Commitment:** Nepal's practice of making incremental, reversible commitments to multiple BRICS partners, avoiding the lock-in that would foreclose future balancing options.

This model draws on but extends the existing small state literature by incorporating the resource diplomacy dimension and situating it within the specific context of Himalayan geopolitics.

### 3.3 MNE Theoretical Lens

From an MNE/International Business perspective, Nepal's resource sector engagement by BRICS-affiliated entities reflects the "eclectic paradigm" of foreign direct investment (Dunning, 1988; Dunning & Lundan, 2008). BRICS-affiliated state-owned enterprises investing in Nepal's hydropower sector demonstrate classic OLI (Ownership, Location, Internationalisation) advantages: technological expertise in large-scale dam construction (Ownership advantage), Nepal's unique resource endowments (Location advantage), and the strategic value of controlling energy supply chains (Internationalisation advantages).

However, these BRICS SOEs also operate under distinctive political economy logics that diverge from conventional MNE theory: their investment decisions are shaped not only by commercial returns but by state strategic objectives, including energy security, geopolitical influence, and diplomatic leverage (Leutert, 2019; Cuervo-Cazurra et al., 2014). This political-commercial hybrid logic makes standard MNE analytical frameworks partially applicable but requires supplementation with international relations theory.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 India's Engagement: Hegemonic Presence and Hydropower Competition

India constitutes Nepal's most consequential BRICS partner, reflecting the two countries' deep historical, cultural, linguistic, and economic ties. India accounts for approximately 65% of Nepal's total trade and serves as Nepal's primary energy export market — a relationship of deep economic interdependency that simultaneously provides Nepal with development opportunities and exposes it to significant structural vulnerability (Government of Nepal, Ministry of Finance, 2023).

#### 4.1.1 Hydropower as Diplomatic Instrument: The Arun-3 Case

India's flagship hydropower engagement in Nepal is the Arun-3 Hydroelectric Project (900 MW), developed by SJVN Limited — a joint venture of the Government of India and the Government of Himachal Pradesh. The Arun-3 project, situated in the Sankhuwasabha district of eastern Nepal, was awarded to SJVN through a survey licence in 2008, with the Investment Board Nepal granting the development agreement in 2018 following protracted negotiations (SJVN Limited, 2022).

The Arun-3 development agreement exemplifies competitive leveraging dynamics: India's ultimate concession of a 22% free equity stake to the Government of Nepal, along with commitments to develop 500 km of roads and a 180 MW distribution system for local areas, reflected in part the pressure exerted by China's competing interest in Arun-3 and adjacent river basins. China's CGGC had conducted feasibility studies on the nearby Arun-4 project, creating a competitive dynamic that strengthened Nepal's negotiating position (Adhikari, 2020; Khadka, 2018).

The Power Trade Agreement (PTA) signed between Nepal and India in 2014 — and its subsequent amendment in 2021 to allow third-country power trading — represents a significant milestone in Nepal's hydropower diplomacy. The 2021 amendment, permitting Nepal to sell electricity through India to Bangladesh, opened a new market dimension that enhanced the commercial viability of Nepal's hydropower sector and reduced its exclusive dependence on the Indian energy market (Nepal Electricity Authority, 2022).

#### 4.1.2 India-Nepal Connectivity and Cross-Border Infrastructure

Beyond hydropower, India has made significant investments in Nepal's surface connectivity infrastructure, partly in response to China's BRI commitments. The Integrated Check Posts (ICP) programme — financed by India at key Nepal-India border points including Birgunj, Biratnagar, Bhairahawa, and Nepalgunj — has modernised Nepal's border infrastructure and facilitated expanded trade flows. The Raxaul-Kathmandu rail project, long promised and repeatedly delayed, gained new momentum from 2020 onward as India sought to counter China's Kerung-Kathmandu railway proposal with its own connectivity narrative (Indian Embassy Kathmandu, 2021).

India's EXIM Bank financing for Nepal's postal highway expansion and the Hulaki Highway — connecting remote Terai districts — reflects a broader Indian strategy of using infrastructure financing to consolidate its influence in Nepal's southern plains (Terai) while China focuses on northern (Himalayan) connectivity. This geographic division of influence, while reflecting historical patterns, is increasingly contested as both powers seek comprehensive connectivity presence.

#### 4.1.3 Strategic Assessment of Indian Engagement

India's engagement in Nepal, while economically dominant, has been complicated by periodic political tensions — most notably the 2015-2016 economic blockade, during which India restricted fuel and goods supplies to Nepal in the context of the Madhesi political dispute (Murton, Lord & Beazley, 2016). This episode was a watershed in Nepal's strategic calculations: it demonstrated the risks of over-dependence on India and catalysed Nepal's accelerated engagement with China and the BRI framework. From a small state strategy perspective, the 2015-2016 crisis validated the Himalayan Balancing Model's prescription of multilateral anchoring as a hedge against bilateral coercion.

### 4.2 China's Engagement: BRI and the New Connectivity Architecture

China's engagement with Nepal has expanded dramatically since the 2015 earthquake and has accelerated following Nepal's BRI accession in May 2017. China's strategy in Nepal operates across three primary dimensions: connectivity infrastructure, trade facilitation, and development financing — all of which intersect with Nepal's resource diplomacy agenda.

#### 4.2.1 Belt and Road Initiative: Projects and Progress

Nepal's BRI engagement encompasses nine priority projects across connectivity, energy, and economic corridor development. The flagship project — and the most geopolitically consequential — is the Kerung-Kathmandu Cross-Border Railway. Proposed as a 72-kilometre extension of the existing Lhasa-Shigatse-Kerung railway from the China-Nepal border at Rasuwagadhi to Kathmandu, this project would be a transformative development: Nepal would become the first Himalayan state with rail connectivity to China, potentially reducing its transit dependence on India (Wang, 2019; Huang, 2016).

The Kerung-Kathmandu Railway has been the subject of extensive feasibility studies, with a detailed feasibility report submitted by China Railway Fifth Survey and Design Institute Group in 2020. However, as of 2025, the project remains in the pre-construction phase, with significant financial, technical, and environmental challenges unresolved. The estimated cost of USD 2.7-3.2 billion represents approximately 25-30% of Nepal's GDP at 2020 values, raising acute debt sustainability concerns (Adhikari, 2020; Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, 2021).

Beyond the railway, China has financed and constructed the Pokhara International Airport — Nepal's third international airport, completed in January 2023 at a cost of USD 215.96 million financed through Chinese EXIM Bank loans. The airport, intended to facilitate tourist access to the Annapurna and Dhaulagiri mountain regions, has faced controversy regarding its commercial viability and debt repayment terms (Nepal Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2023).

#### 4.2.2 Hydropower: China's Competitive Entry

China's engagement in Nepal's hydropower sector has served both commercial and geopolitical functions. The Budhi Gandaki Hydropower Project (1,200 MW), one of Nepal's priority storage hydropower projects, was initially allocated to China Gezhouba Group Corporation (CGGC) in 2016 through a government-to-government agreement. However, the project was subsequently cancelled in 2018 amid controversy over procurement processes, before being re-awarded to China Gezhouba Group Corporation following domestic political debate (Khadka, 2018; Bhattarai, 2021).

The Budhi Gandaki case illustrates the complex political economy of Nepal's hydropower diplomacy. The project's cancellation and subsequent re-award reflected domestic political pressures, anti-China sentiment in some quarters, and Indian concerns about Chinese control of a major Nepalese river. The episode demonstrates that Nepal's competitive leveraging strategy involves managing not only international relationships but also domestic political coalitions — a dimension often neglected in international relations scholarship focused on state-level strategic behaviour.

China PowerChina's development of the West Seti Hydropower Project (750 MW), and CGGC's involvement in the Upper Marsyangdi-2 project, further extend Chinese presence in Nepal's hydropower sector. These projects, collectively representing over 2,000 MW of potential capacity, constitute a significant competitive counter to Indian hydropower dominance (Nepal Water and Energy Development Company, 2022).

#### 4.2.3 Trade and Economic Corridors

China has progressively expanded trade access points on the Nepal-Tibet border, with Rasuwagadhi, Tatopani (reopened in 2019 following 2015 earthquake damage), and Olangchung Gola customs points providing multiple routes for Chinese goods. In 2016, Nepal and China signed a Trade and Transit Agreement establishing Nepal's right to use Chinese seaports at Tianjin, Shenzhen, Lianyungang, and Zhanjiang — theoretically liberating Nepal from complete dependence on the Indian port of Kolkata for third-country trade. While actual utilisation of Chinese seaports remains limited due to logistics costs and infrastructure constraints, the agreement represents a significant symbolic and diplomatic achievement (Murton, Lord & Beazley, 2016).

### 4.3 New Development Bank: Multilateral Financing and Nepal's Aspirations

The New Development Bank (NDB), established by BRICS members in 2015, represents a qualitatively different dimension of Nepal's BRICS engagement — one involving multilateral rather than bilateral financing architecture. Nepal is not a founding NDB member, but its growing interest in NDB financing reflects the institution's strategic positioning as an alternative to traditional multilateral development banks.

#### 4.3.1 NDB's Evolving Membership and Nepal's Access

As of 2024, the NDB has expanded its membership beyond the original BRICS five to include Bangladesh, Egypt, the UAE, Uruguay, and Ethiopia. Nepal has engaged with NDB mechanisms through co-financing arrangements and observer status discussions. The NDB's lending portfolio, which exceeded USD 30 billion in commitments by 2024, has focused predominantly on infrastructure, clean energy, and urban development — all areas of high relevance to Nepal's development priorities (New Development Bank, 2024).

The NDB's infrastructure financing model — which generally charges market-adjacent interest rates but without the governance conditionalities associated with World Bank or ADB lending — presents Nepal with an additional financing option that could supplement bilateral Chinese and Indian investment. For Nepal's hydropower sector in particular, the NDB's clean energy mandate aligns closely with Nepal's development priorities (Humphrey, 2015).

#### 4.3.2 Contingent Reserve Arrangement and Monetary Stabilisation

The BRICS Contingent Reserve Arrangement (CRA), a USD 100 billion financial safety net analogous to the IMF's Special Drawing Rights mechanism, is relevant to Nepal's monetary stability in a more indirect sense. Nepal's currency peg to the Indian Rupee means that rupee volatility — which is itself influenced by India's BRICS financial relationships — has downstream effects on Nepal's macroeconomic stability. Nepal's ability to access alternative financial stabilisation mechanisms, even indirectly, represents a component of its multilateral anchoring strategy.

### 4.4 Other BRICS Members: Brazil, Russia, and South Africa

While India and China dominate Nepal's BRICS engagement, the other three original BRICS members — Brazil, Russia, and South Africa — play secondary but non-trivial roles in Nepal's resource diplomacy context.

**Brazil:** Brazil's most significant contribution to Nepal's development trajectory comes through its expertise in large-scale hydropower development. Brazilian companies and Brazilian technical expertise, particularly in the Itaipu and Belo Monte dam management models, have informed Nepal's hydropower sector management frameworks. Brazil's Embraer has supplied aircraft to Nepal Airlines Corporation, representing a commercial engagement that, while modest, reflects South-South business linkages (Nepal Airlines Corporation, 2020).

**Russia:** Russia's engagement with Nepal has been relatively modest in material terms but significant in political-diplomatic terms. Russia's consistent support for Nepal's sovereignty claims — particularly regarding the Lipulekh, Kalapani, and Limpiyadhura territorial disputes with India — in United Nations forums represents a form of diplomatic capital that Nepal has occasionally leveraged. Russia has also engaged Nepal through military equipment supply (Russian-designed armaments in Nepal Army) and educational scholarships (Thapa, 2018).

**South Africa:** South Africa's engagement with Nepal is primarily channelled through UN peacekeeping coordination — Nepal is one of the world's largest troop contributors to UN peacekeeping operations — and through Commonwealth-adjacent development cooperation frameworks. South Africa's technical assistance in governance and anti-corruption frameworks has supplemented Nepal's domestic reform processes (Upreti, 2016).

### 4.5 Nepal's Strategic Responses: Competitive Leveraging in Practice

Nepal's strategic responses to BRICS member competition can be organised around four empirically observable patterns:

#### 4.5.1 Competitive Licensing and Re-Licensing

Nepal's Investment Board has demonstrated a pattern of using the prospect of alternative BRICS investor engagement to renegotiate project terms with existing license holders. The most notable example is the Upper Karnali Hydropower Project (900 MW): initially licensed to GMR Group of India in 2008, the project was subsequently subjected to repeated renegotiation, with Nepal using Chinese interest in the Karnali basin to extract improved terms from GMR, including enhanced free power provisions, road connectivity commitments, and employment quotas for Nepali nationals (Khadka, 2018; Bhattarai, 2021).

#### 4.5.2 Multilateral Institutional Anchoring

Nepal has strategically joined multiple multilateral frameworks — including the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB, of which China is the largest shareholder), the South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC, where India is dominant), and the Bangladesh-China-India-Myanmar Economic Corridor (BCIM) — to create institutional buffers against bilateral dominance. Nepal's application for AIIB membership (approved in 2016) provides access to Chinese-led multilateral financing with somewhat more transparent governance than bilateral Chinese EXIM Bank loans (Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, 2021).

#### 4.5.3 Sequenced Bilateral Commitments

Nepal's practice of signing framework agreements and memoranda of understanding with multiple BRICS members on overlapping issue areas — without converting all agreements into binding legal commitments — allows Nepal to maintain strategic flexibility. The BRI Framework Agreement of 2017, for example, committed Nepal to the BRI concept without specifying projects, timelines, or financing terms, preserving Nepal's ability to prioritise projects selectively and negotiate terms bilaterally for each initiative (Adhikari, 2020).

#### 4.5.4 Domestic Political Coalition Management

Nepal's federal democratic political system introduces a layer of complexity into its resource diplomacy that pure IR theory tends to underestimate. Different political parties have varying degrees of alignment with India (Nepali Congress) and China (Communist Party of Nepal), and government changes have periodically resulted in shifts in hydropower project priorities, renegotiations of Chinese commitments, and recalibrations of Indian engagement. This domestic political variability, while creating implementation challenges, also provides Nepal's governments with plausible domestic political cover for strategic renegotiations that serve the state's long-term balancing interests (Ogura, 2008; Thapa, 2018).

### 4.6 Development Outcomes: Achievements and Structural Risks

#### 4.6.1 Positive Development Outcomes

Nepal's competitive engagement with BRICS resource diplomacy has generated measurable positive development outcomes. Installed hydropower capacity grew from approximately 800 MW in 2015 to over 2,400 MW by 2023, a three-fold increase largely financed through BRICS-affiliated investment and financing mechanisms (Nepal Electricity Authority, 2023). The Upper Tamakoshi Hydroelectric Project (456 MW), while primarily domestically financed, benefited from Chinese construction expertise and Indian technical consulting, demonstrating productive multilateral engagement even in Nepal-led projects.

Road connectivity in Nepal's northern (Himalayan) districts has improved substantially through BRI-linked and Chinese grant financing, opening new market access and tourism development opportunities. The Araniko Highway rehabilitation and the Hilsa-Simikot road, both involving Chinese and Indian financing respectively, illustrate the geographic spread of BRICS-affiliated infrastructure development.

#### 4.6.2 Structural Risks and Vulnerabilities

Significant structural risks accompany Nepal's engagement with BRICS resource diplomacy. Debt sustainability concerns are paramount: Nepal's external debt stock reached approximately NPR 1.2 trillion (approximately USD 9 billion) by 2023, with the share of concessional debt from traditional donors declining relative to market-rate commercial borrowing from Chinese EXIM Bank (Government of Nepal, Ministry of Finance, 2023). The Pokhara Airport's low passenger volumes — significantly below projections — have raised questions about debt repayment capacity and the commercial rationale underlying project selection.

Asymmetric power relations constitute a second structural vulnerability. Despite Nepal's competitive leveraging strategy, the fundamental power asymmetry between Nepal and its BRICS neighbours means that Nepal's bargaining leverage is constrained by its ultimate dependence on Indian transit access and Chinese investment flows. The 2015-2016 blockade demonstrated that India retains significant coercive capacity over Nepal's economy that cannot be fully hedged through Chinese engagement alone.

Environmental and social risks from large-scale hydropower development represent a third vulnerability. Several proposed hydropower projects in Nepal involve significant population displacement, seismic risk given Nepal's earthquake-prone geology, and downstream environmental impacts. International advocacy organisations have raised concerns about governance of environmental impact assessments for BRI-linked projects specifically, noting reduced transparency compared to World Bank-financed equivalents (International Rivers, 2020).

## 5. Research Contributions and Remaining Gaps

### 5.1 Contributions of This Paper

This paper makes four distinct contributions to existing scholarship:

**First,** it establishes Nepal as a substantive case study in BRICS scholarship, addressing the near-total absence of Nepal-focused analysis in a literature dominated by African and Latin American cases. Nepal's unique geographic position between two BRICS giants provides an analytically distinctive vantage point that generates insights not replicable from other case studies.

**Second,** it introduces the conceptual innovation of "supplier-side resource diplomacy" — the idea that resource-holding developing states can employ their natural assets as active diplomatic instruments rather than merely receiving the attentions of resource-seeking great powers. This concept extends and enriches the existing resource diplomacy literature, which has been predominantly concerned with resource-seeking strategies of importing states.

**Third,** it proposes the Himalayan Balancing Model (HBM) as a theoretical framework for understanding small state agency in multipolar geopolitical environments. The HBM's four components — resource niche identification, competitive leveraging, multilateral anchoring, and incremental commitment — provide an analytically tractable framework that can be applied to other small, resource-endowed states navigating great power competition.

**Fourth,** it integrates MNE/International Business theory with international relations scholarship to analyse BRICS-affiliated SOEs in Nepal's resource sector. This theoretical integration is underdeveloped in both IB literature (which tends to treat MNEs as primarily commercial actors) and IR literature (which tends to treat states as monolithic strategic actors), and the Nepal case provides rich empirical material for advancing this synthesis.

### 5.2 Remaining Gaps and Future Research Agenda

Despite this paper's contributions, significant research gaps remain. **Quantitative impact assessment** of BRICS-affiliated investments on Nepal's development indicators — GDP growth, energy access, trade volumes, poverty rates — requires systematic econometric analysis that lies beyond this paper's qualitative scope. Future research should employ difference-in-differences or synthetic control methods to isolate BRICS investment effects from other development drivers.

**Sub-national dimensions** of Nepal's resource diplomacy — particularly the differential impacts on mountain, hill, and Terai communities; on ethnic and caste groups; and on gender equity — remain largely unexamined. The distribution of development benefits from hydropower projects, in particular, raises important social equity questions that national-level analysis obscures.

**The post-2024 BRICS expansion** and its implications for Nepal deserve dedicated scholarly attention. The addition of Egypt, Ethiopia, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE — all with interests in renewable energy, infrastructure, and South Asian connectivity — creates new dimensions in Nepal's resource diplomacy options that this paper cannot fully address.

**Comparative analysis** of Nepal's strategy relative to other small Himalayan states — Bhutan, which has pursued a very different strategy of deep Indian alignment and hydropower export, and Sri Lanka, which experienced severe debt distress from Chinese BRI investments — would enrich understanding of the HBM's applicability and limitations.

## 6. Conclusion and Policy Implications

### 6.1 Summary of Findings

This paper has examined Nepal's strategic navigation of BRICS resource diplomacy across the period 2015-2025, revealing a country that has evolved from relative passivity in international economic diplomacy to active, if constrained, strategic agency. Nepal's hydropower resources, vast but as yet largely undeveloped, have emerged as the primary currency of its BRICS resource diplomacy — attracting competing Indian, Chinese, and broader BRICS-affiliated investments, creating bargaining leverage that Nepal has increasingly learned to deploy.

The analysis demonstrates that Nepal has pursued a coherent, if not always explicitly articulated, competitive leveraging strategy: cultivating competing BRICS interests, anchoring its engagements in multilateral frameworks, and maintaining incremental commitments that preserve strategic flexibility. This strategy has generated measurable development gains — particularly in installed hydropower capacity, northern connectivity, and border trade infrastructure — while also exposing Nepal to structural vulnerabilities in debt sustainability, environmental governance, and asymmetric power dependence.

### 6.2 Policy Implications for Nepal

**For Nepalese Policymakers:**

1. **Institutionalise Resource Diplomacy:** Nepal should establish a dedicated Resource Diplomacy Coordination Unit within the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, tasked with coordinating hydropower licensing, financing negotiations, and project monitoring across all BRICS partners in a coherent strategic framework. Currently, this coordination is fragmented across multiple ministries and agencies, reducing Nepal's negotiating coherence.

2. **Strengthen Debt Sustainability Frameworks:** Nepal should adopt a formal Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA) requirement for all project financing exceeding USD 50 million, with independent review mechanisms analogous to the IMF's Debt Sustainability Framework. This would provide early warning of unsustainable debt accumulation from market-rate BRICS financing, particularly Chinese EXIM Bank loans.

3. **Diversify Energy Export Markets:** Nepal should prioritise the operationalisation of third-country electricity trading — including exports to Bangladesh and potentially beyond — to reduce dependence on India as the sole export market for hydropower. Multilateral energy trading frameworks within the SAARC or BIMSTEC architecture would enhance Nepal's resource diplomacy leverage by expanding the market for its primary strategic asset.

4. **Leverage NDB Membership:** Nepal should actively pursue full NDB membership or associate member status, which would provide access to multilateral development financing with BRICS support but with greater governance transparency than bilateral Chinese or Indian financing. NDB membership would also serve as an additional multilateral anchor in Nepal's BRICS engagement.

5. **Prioritise Seismic-Safe Hydropower Development:** Given Nepal's acute seismic vulnerability — demonstrated catastrophically in the 2015 Gorkha earthquake — Nepal should insist on international best-practice seismic safety standards for all BRICS-affiliated hydropower projects as a non-negotiable licensing condition. This requirement would also serve as a quality signal that differentiates Nepal's regulatory environment from less demanding competitors.

**For MNEs and International Investors:**

Nepal's BRICS resource diplomacy engagement creates both opportunities and risks for international commercial actors. The competitive dynamic between Indian and Chinese state-owned enterprises creates space for third-country private sector participation in hydropower and infrastructure — particularly in project management, technology supply, and environmental services — where neither Indian nor Chinese firms hold decisive advantages. MNEs with expertise in high-altitude construction, hydroelectric turbine manufacturing, and environmental impact management should view Nepal's growing hydropower sector as a commercially attractive niche market.

However, political risk management is essential. Nepal's domestic political volatility — including frequent government changes, parliamentary debates over project allocations, and periodic nationalist mobilisations — creates implementation uncertainty that commercial investors must price into project viability assessments. Political risk insurance through multilateral guarantors (MIGA, ADB's credit guarantee facilities) should be considered standard practice for large-scale Nepal resource sector investments.

### 6.3 Broader Lessons for Small State Resource Diplomacy

Nepal's experience offers broader lessons for other small, resource-endowed states navigating great power competition in the twenty-first century. The core lesson is that resource endowments, even when as yet undeveloped, can function as effective diplomatic instruments when a state possesses the institutional capacity to manage competing interests strategically. The critical prerequisites for successful supplier-side resource diplomacy are: coherent domestic governance of resource licensing; multilateral institutional anchoring to constrain bilateral dominance; sequential commitment strategies that preserve flexibility; and robust debt sustainability frameworks to prevent resource assets from being converted into financial liabilities.

Nepal's trajectory between 2015 and 2025 demonstrates that small states are not merely passive objects of great power competition but active, if constrained, agents in the geopolitical processes that shape their development trajectories. The BRICS framework — with its inherent inter-member tensions, particularly between India and China — provides structural opportunities for small states to extract development gains that would be unavailable in a unipolar geopolitical environment. Nepal's strategic navigation of this complex landscape, with its successes, failures, and ongoing negotiations, constitutes a significant case study for scholars of international development, international business, and small state strategic behaviour.

### 6.4 Final Reflections

As BRICS continues to expand and evolve — potentially incorporating new energy producers and emerging economies — Nepal's position at the nexus of Asian connectivity and Himalayan resource wealth will only grow in strategic significance. The 2024 BRICS expansion, bringing in Gulf energy producers alongside African and Latin American economies, hints at a global economic architecture in which the existing Bretton Woods order faces sustained challenge. For Nepal, this multipolar emerging order represents both opportunity — more diverse financing and investment options — and risk — greater geopolitical pressure from competing great powers seeking to secure Himalayan resource access and strategic positioning.

Nepal's challenge is to translate its geopolitical centrality into sustainable development outcomes that benefit its citizens across all regions, communities, and social strata. Resource diplomacy, while a powerful tool, is ultimately a means to an end: the construction of a prosperous, equitable, and resilient Nepalese society. Ensuring that BRICS resource diplomacy serves this ultimate objective — rather than becoming an end in itself or a vehicle for elite capture — remains the most important policy challenge facing Nepalese policymakers in the decade ahead.
