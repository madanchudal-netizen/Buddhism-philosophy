## Executive Summary

This article examines Nepal's strategic navigation of BRICS resource diplomacy and evaluates its impact on Nepal's national development trajectory between 2015 and 2025. Nepal, uniquely positioned between two BRICS giants — India and China — has increasingly engaged with the BRICS institutional ecosystem, including the New Development Bank (NDB) and the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), while simultaneously maintaining its traditional diplomatic balancing act. The paper argues that Nepal has evolved from a passive recipient of foreign aid into an active diplomatic agent leveraging competition among BRICS members — particularly in hydropower, water resources, and mineral sectors — to extract greater development concessions. Through the lens of resource diplomacy and small state strategic theory, this article demonstrates that Nepal's resource endowments, particularly its vast hydropower potential (~83,000 MW), function as geopolitical currency in its negotiations with BRICS members. Empirical analysis of flagship projects — including the Upper Tamakoshi Hydroelectric Project (456 MW), Budhi Gandaki Hydropower Project (1,200 MW), Arun-3 Hydropower Project (900 MW), and BRI-linked connectivity projects — reveals a pattern of strategic utilization of inter-BRICS competition. However, the paper also identifies significant structural risks: asymmetric power relations, debt sustainability concerns, and implementation gaps. Policy recommendations are provided for Nepalese policymakers to maximise development gains while mitigating geopolitical vulnerabilities.

## Abstract

Nepal's position as a landlocked Himalayan state sandwiched between two BRICS members — India and China — provides a unique analytical lens through which to examine small state agency in resource diplomacy. This article investigates the central research question: **How has Nepal strategically navigated BRICS resource diplomacy to advance its national development objectives between 2015 and 2025?** Employing a qualitative case study methodology supplemented by secondary data from government reports, multilateral institutional documents, and academic literature, this study analyses Nepal's engagement across four dimensions: hydropower diplomacy, multilateral financing through the New Development Bank (NDB), Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) connectivity projects, and South-South cooperation frameworks.

The findings reveal that Nepal has adopted a "competitive leveraging" strategy — deliberately cultivating competing BRICS interests in its hydropower sector and transit corridors to negotiate better project terms, financing conditions, and technology transfers. Specific case studies including the Arun-3 Hydropower Project (India, 900 MW), the Budhi Gandaki Project (China, 1,200 MW), and BRI-linked Kerung-Kathmandu railway negotiations demonstrate both the opportunities and structural limitations of this approach. The paper identifies a significant research gap in existing scholarship: while bilateral Nepal–India and Nepal–China relations are well-documented, the *multilateral* dimension of Nepal's BRICS engagement and Nepal's proactive agency therein remain virtually unexamined.

This paper contributes to the nascent literature on small state resource diplomacy in Asia, extends BRICS scholarship beyond Africa-centric cases, and offers actionable policy frameworks for Nepalese development planners and Multinational Enterprises (MNEs) operating in the Himalayan resource sector. Theoretical contributions include a novel "Himalayan Balancing Model" (HBM) — a four-component framework of resource niche identification, competitive leveraging, multilateral anchoring, and incremental commitment — applicable to other small, resource-rich states navigating great power competition.

**Keywords:** Nepal, BRICS, Resource Diplomacy, Hydropower, New Development Bank, Belt and Road Initiative, Small State Agency, Geopolitical Balancing, South Asian Development, Multinational Enterprises

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Nepal's Geostrategic Significance

Nepal occupies a singular position in contemporary Asian geopolitics. A landlocked nation of approximately 30 million people, Nepal is bordered to the north by the Tibet Autonomous Region of China and on three remaining sides by India — two of the most consequential emerging powers in the twenty-first century global order and both core members of the BRICS grouping (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa). This geographic reality, which has historically been experienced as a constraint by Nepalese policymakers, has increasingly been reframed in Nepalese foreign policy discourse as a strategic asset: the country sits at the nexus of two civilisational powers with deep historical interests in Himalayan resources, trade routes, and political influence (Muni, 2012; Upreti, 2016). As Baral (2012, p. 34) observes, Nepal's geographic situation "creates both its greatest vulnerability and, potentially, its most significant diplomatic leverage."

The period from 2015 to 2025 has been particularly transformative for Nepal's engagement with BRICS-aligned resource diplomacy. Following the devastating 2015 Gorkha earthquake, which killed nearly 9,000 people and caused economic damage estimated at USD 7.1 billion — approximately one-third of Nepal's GDP at the time — Nepal's political leadership increasingly recognised the strategic value of its hydropower potential as both a development resource and a diplomatic instrument (Upreti, 2016; World Bank, 2015). With an estimated technically feasible hydropower capacity of 83,000 megawatts (MW) — of which less than 2,500 MW had been harnessed as of 2020 — Nepal possesses resource endowments that have attracted intense interest from Indian, Chinese, and other BRICS-affiliated investors (International Energy Agency, 2021; Nepal Electricity Authority, 2022). The International Energy Agency (2021, p. 12) characterises Nepal as possessing "among the highest concentrations of undeveloped hydropower potential per unit of territory in Asia," a designation that frames Nepal's resource endowments as a geopolitical asset of the first order.

The 2015 earthquake and its aftermath proved to be a strategic inflection point in Nepal's diplomatic posture. When a prolonged economic blockade imposed by India in 2015–2016 — ostensibly triggered by Nepal's constitutional process but widely attributed to Indian pressure over the treatment of Madhesi communities — cut fuel supplies to Nepal for approximately five months, Nepal's ruling government under Prime Minister K.P. Sharma Oli turned to China for emergency fuel deliveries and accelerated diplomatic engagement (Murton, Lord & Beazley, 2016). This episode fundamentally altered Nepal's strategic calculus: the risks of over-dependence on any single great power neighbour became viscerally apparent, and Nepal's engagement with the BRICS framework — particularly the Belt and Road Initiative and the New Development Bank — acquired a new strategic urgency rooted not merely in development aspirations but in geopolitical risk diversification.

### 1.2 The BRICS Framework and Small State Engagement

The BRICS grouping, while not a formal multilateral organisation comparable to the United Nations or World Trade Organisation, has evolved into a significant framework for South-South cooperation, alternative development financing, and geopolitical signalling (Stuenkel, 2020; Prashad, 2013). Originally conceptualised by Goldman Sachs economist Jim O'Neill (2001) as an economic category projecting the rise of Brazil, Russia, India, and China, the grouping has evolved — with the addition of South Africa in 2010 and six further members in 2024 — into a geopolitical project with genuine institutional architecture (Armijo, 2007; Wilson & Purushothaman, 2003).

The establishment of the New Development Bank (NDB) in 2015 — headquartered in Shanghai with initial capitalisation of USD 50 billion and authorised capital of USD 100 billion — provided BRICS with a concrete institutional mechanism for financing infrastructure and development projects beyond the traditional Bretton Woods architecture (New Development Bank, 2024). For Nepal, this development opened new pathways for development financing not conditioned by the stringent governance and structural adjustment requirements associated with World Bank or International Monetary Fund lending (Humphrey, 2015). As Humphrey (2015, p. 97) notes, the NDB's founding documents explicitly reject "governance conditionality as a standard lending requirement," positioning it as a structurally distinct alternative to Bretton Woods institutions.

Simultaneously, China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI), which Nepal formally joined in May 2017 through a Framework Agreement with China, introduced a new dimension to Nepal's resource diplomacy calculus. The BRI promised transformative connectivity infrastructure — most dramatically, a trans-Himalayan railway connecting Lhasa to Kathmandu via the Kerung border point — that could fundamentally alter Nepal's landlocked status and open new economic corridors (Wang, 2019; Huang, 2016). Wang (2019, p. 458) characterises the BRI as an "offensive for defensive" strategy in which China "uses economic statecraft to construct a favourable strategic environment" — a framing that underscores the geopolitical dimensions of China's infrastructure investments in Nepal beyond their stated commercial rationale. The geopolitical implications of this potential connectivity were not lost on India, Nepal's dominant trading partner and the source of approximately 65% of Nepal's imports, which responded with its own enhanced infrastructure commitments and renewed engagement on bilateral hydropower projects (Pant, 2017; Indian Embassy Kathmandu, 2021).

### 1.3 Significance of the Research

The interaction between Nepal's resource endowments and the competitive interests of BRICS members represents a microcosm of broader trends in twenty-first century development geopolitics. As BRICS continues to expand — admitting Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Ethiopia, Iran, Egypt, and Argentina in 2024 — the framework's influence on developing countries' strategic choices is growing rapidly. Nepal's experience offers instructive lessons for other small, resource-endowed states navigating multipolar great power competition (Stuenkel, 2020).

From a Multinational Enterprises (MNE) perspective, Nepal's resource sector presents a particularly rich case study of how geopolitical competition shapes investment environments, risk landscapes, and business opportunities for international corporations. State-owned enterprises (SOEs) from BRICS members — including India's SJVN Limited, NHPC Limited, and China's PowerChina, China Gezhouba Group Corporation (CGGC) — operate as quasi-MNEs in Nepal's energy sector, blurring the conventional distinction between commercial investment and geopolitical statecraft (Leutert, 2019; Thacker, 2022; Cuervo-Cazurra et al., 2014). Thacker (2022, p. 45) describes this phenomenon as "SOE-mediated resource diplomacy," in which state enterprises serve simultaneously as commercial actors pursuing returns and as diplomatic instruments pursuing national strategic objectives.

This paper makes four original contributions to existing scholarship: (1) it establishes Nepal as a substantive BRICS case study; (2) it develops the concept of "supplier-side resource diplomacy"; (3) it proposes the Himalayan Balancing Model (HBM) as a novel theoretical framework; and (4) it integrates MNE theory with IR scholarship on small state strategy. The article proceeds as follows: Section 2 reviews existing scholarship on BRICS, resource diplomacy, and South Asian geopolitics; Section 3 presents the conceptual framework; Section 4 provides the analytical core; Section 5 identifies research contributions and remaining gaps; and Section 6 concludes with policy implications.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 BRICS Scholarship: Evolution and Debates

The academic literature on BRICS has grown substantially since O'Neill's (2001) original BRIC formulation. Early scholarship focused predominantly on BRICS as an economic phenomenon — projecting the collective economic rise of the four original economies and their implications for the global balance of economic power (Wilson & Purushothaman, 2003; Armijo, 2007). Wilson and Purushothaman (2003, p. 4) projected that the BRIC economies would "collectively surpass the G6 in US dollar terms by 2039," a projection that captured global imagination and invested the BRIC category with political significance beyond its analytic origins.

Subsequent scholarship broadened to examine BRICS as a geopolitical project challenging Western hegemony in international institutions, trade regimes, and financial architectures (Stuenkel, 2020; Pant, 2013; Prashad, 2013). Stuenkel (2020, p. 3) argues that BRICS has evolved from "a Goldman Sachs marketing concept to a genuine geopolitical actor," evidenced by the NDB's operational lending portfolio, the Contingent Reserve Arrangement, and BRICS summit declarations that increasingly challenge the legitimacy of Western-dominated global governance. Pant (2013), however, offers a sceptical counterpoint, characterising BRICS coherence as limited by fundamental strategic divergences among members — particularly between India and China — that constrain collective action and reduce the grouping's efficacy as a strategic vehicle.

A significant body of literature has examined BRICS engagement with the African continent, where grouping members — particularly China, India, and Brazil — have made substantial infrastructure investments tied to natural resource extraction (Carmody, 2011; Taylor, 2014; Alden, 2007). Taylor (2014) provides a critical account of what he terms "diversifying dependency" in Africa-BRICS relations, arguing that while BRICS members have provided African states with additional development financing options, the fundamental structure of resource extraction and infrastructure investment has replicated rather than transformed colonial-era economic relations. These Africa-focused studies have generated sophisticated theoretical frameworks for understanding BRICS resource diplomacy, but as Wang (2019) notes, these frameworks have been inadequately applied to South Asian contexts, where the geopolitical dynamics differ substantially.

The most analytically comprehensive account of BRICS institutions remains Stuenkel's (2020) study, which traces the evolution from informal diplomatic coordination to the establishment of the NDB, the Contingent Reserve Arrangement (CRA), and the BRICS Business Council. Stuenkel argues that inter-member tensions — particularly between India and China, which fought a border conflict in the Galwan Valley in 2020 — have periodically undermined BRICS coherence but paradoxically created opportunities for smaller states to exploit inter-member competition. This paradox is central to understanding Nepal's strategic position.

### 2.2 Resource Diplomacy: Theoretical Foundations

The concept of resource diplomacy has been developed across international relations, development studies, and international political economy scholarship. Klare (2012, p. 27) provides a foundational account of how competition for scarce natural resources — oil, water, minerals, and increasingly renewable energy sources — has become "a structural driver of twenty-first century geopolitics," asserting that resource competition will increasingly determine the character of great power relations. Ross (2012) and Humphreys, Sachs, and Stiglitz (2007) examine the "resource curse" hypothesis, documenting how resource wealth can paradoxically retard development when governance institutions are weak — a concern directly relevant to Nepal's emerging hydropower sector.

Hydropower diplomacy constitutes a relatively nascent but rapidly growing subfield within resource diplomacy scholarship. Mirumachi (2015, p. 3) argues that transboundary water resources create "complex interdependencies between upstream and downstream states, generating both conflict potential and cooperation incentives that operate simultaneously rather than sequentially." This simultaneous conflict-cooperation dynamic, which Mirumachi terms "Transboundary Waters Interaction Nexus" (TWINS), is directly applicable to Nepal's position at the headwaters of rivers flowing into India — the Koshi, Gandaki, and Karnali — which constitute both shared resources and geopolitical leverage points.

Zeitoun and Warner (2006, p. 436) develop a "hydro-hegemony" framework that identifies how powerful riparian states assert "structural, ideational, and discursive power" over water resource allocation in ways that disadvantage weaker riparian states. Shrestha (2018, p. 790) applies this framework directly to Nepal, documenting how "India's downstream position and its leverage over Nepalese energy exports has historically constrained Nepal's hydropower development options through both formal treaty provisions and informal diplomatic pressure." The 1996 Mahakali Treaty — widely criticised in Nepal as advantageous to India — is cited as a paradigmatic instance of hydro-hegemonic dynamics (Baral, 2012; Shrestha, 2018).

Chellaney (2011) provides a comprehensive account of China's strategic use of its upstream position on Asian river systems, arguing that China's dam-building programme on the Brahmaputra, Mekong, and Salween rivers constitutes a form of "water nationalism" — the deliberate accumulation of hydraulic leverage over downstream neighbours as a component of strategic competition. While Nepal is not directly a downstream state relative to China on major shared rivers, Chellaney's (2011, p. 203) broader argument — that "water has become Asia's defining resource contest" — frames the geopolitical context within which Nepal's hydropower diplomacy unfolds.

### 2.3 Small State Strategies in International Relations

The theoretical literature on small state strategies in international relations is essential to understanding Nepal's diplomatic options. Keohane (1969, p. 295) established the foundational distinction between "system-affecting states" — those capable of altering the international system through collective action — and "system-ineffectual" small states that must accept the international system as given. Nepal clearly falls into the latter category in terms of material power, but Keohane's framework has been substantially refined by subsequent scholarship that identifies the diverse strategies through which small states nonetheless exercise influence.

Walt's (1987) analysis of alliance behaviour distinguishes between "bandwagoning" — aligning with a dominant power — and "balancing" — building countervailing coalitions against a dominant power. Small states in asymmetric environments often face pressure to bandwagon but may prefer balancing strategies when multiple great powers compete for their alignment (Walt, 1987, p. 17). Nepal's foreign policy between 2015 and 2025 can be read as a sustained attempt to maintain a balancing posture — leveraging China's engagement to prevent excessive Indian dominance, while maintaining Indian connectivity to prevent excessive Chinese dependence (Ogura, 2008; Thapa, 2018). Thapa (2018, p. 45) characterises Nepal's foreign policy as "equidistance diplomacy," a practice of "maintaining roughly equivalent distances from both great power neighbours to maximise strategic autonomy."

Wivel and Thorhallsson (2018) argue that small states increasingly exploit "niche diplomacy" — specialising in specific issue areas where they possess comparative advantages — to exercise influence disproportionate to their material capabilities. Nepal's hydropower resources constitute precisely such a niche: a sector in which Nepal possesses resource abundance that both India and China need, creating structural leverage that transcends Nepal's nominal power deficit. Thorhallsson (2006, p. 12) observes that small states that successfully identify and exploit such niches "can achieve security and prosperity outcomes that significantly exceed what their raw power capabilities would predict."

Hey (2003) catalogues the diverse foreign policy strategies available to small states, including "limited multilateralism," "bilateral balancing," "economic specialisation," and "international institutionalism" — all of which appear in Nepal's BRICS engagement strategy. Hey's (2003, p. 8) observation that small states often "pursue multiple strategies simultaneously rather than sequentially" is borne out by Nepal's concurrent engagement with BRI, NDB, AIIB, SAARC, and bilateral hydropower negotiations.

### 2.4 Nepal-BRICS Studies: Identified Research Gap

Direct academic scholarship on Nepal-BRICS relations is remarkably sparse, constituting the primary gap this paper addresses. Muni (2012) and Baral (2012) provide comprehensive accounts of Nepal's foreign policy, including its engagement with India and China, but both predate the NDB's establishment and Nepal's BRI accession, rendering them analytically incomplete for the post-2015 period. Upreti (2016) examines Nepal's post-earthquake reconstruction and the role of international actors including BRICS members, but from a humanitarian assistance rather than resource diplomacy perspective.

More recent scholarship by Adhikari (2020) and Bhattarai (2021) examines specific bilateral dimensions — Adhikari on Nepal-China BRI engagement, Bhattarai on Nepal-India hydropower diplomacy — but neither frames these within a multilateral BRICS analytical framework. Adhikari (2020, p. 423) acknowledges this limitation explicitly, noting that "the broader BRICS context of Nepal-China relations remains underexplored." Bhattarai (2021, p. 6) similarly flags that Nepal-India hydropower diplomacy needs to be understood "within the broader competitive framework of India-China rivalry rather than purely as a bilateral relationship."

Shrestha (2018) comes closest to the present paper's concerns, examining Nepal's hydropower sector from a geopolitical perspective, but focuses primarily on the India-Nepal dyad and does not engage substantively with China's rising presence or the NDB's potential role. Murton, Lord, and Beazley (2016, p. 405) provide a richly empirical account of Chinese investment in Nepal in the post-earthquake period, but their analysis is sociological rather than strategic, focusing on "how Chinese investment reshapes spatial and political economies at the local level."

The gap is unambiguous: no existing study examines Nepal's *multilateral* engagement with the BRICS ecosystem as a whole, nor has any study systematically analysed Nepal's *proactive strategic agency* in leveraging BRICS inter-member competition for development advantage. This paper addresses both lacunae directly.

## 3. Conceptual Framework

### 3.1 Supplier-Side Resource Diplomacy

This paper employs resource diplomacy as its primary analytical lens, but with an important theoretical modification. In conventional usage, "resource diplomacy" refers to the diplomatic strategies employed by resource-*importing* states to secure access to natural resources held by exporting states (Klare, 2012). This paper argues, however, that resource diplomacy can be conceived as a dual-direction instrument: resource-*holding* states like Nepal can equally employ their resource endowments as diplomatic instruments to attract investment, negotiate favourable terms, and leverage competing interests. This "supplier-side" resource diplomacy inverts the conventional framing while drawing on the same analytical vocabulary.

Supplier-side resource diplomacy operates through three distinct mechanisms:

**Competitive Attraction** — The deliberate cultivation of competing interests from multiple resource-seeking actors, creating a competitive market for resource access rights. When Nepal simultaneously invites Indian, Chinese, and domestic entities to bid for hydropower licenses, it is engaging in competitive attraction (Bhattarai, 2021; Khadka, 2018).

**Conditionality Reciprocity** — Using resource access as a bargaining chip to extract concessions in financing terms, technology transfer, employment provisions, and political agreements. Nepal's negotiation of free power provisions (typically 10–27% of project output depending on project type and duration) from foreign hydropower developers exemplifies conditionality reciprocity (SJVN Limited, 2022; Nepal Water and Energy Development Company, 2022).

**Strategic Sequencing** — Timing resource access negotiations to exploit moments of heightened inter-great-power competition. Nepal's acceleration of BRI Framework Agreement negotiations in 2016–2017, immediately following the Indian economic blockade, exemplifies strategic sequencing: India's coercive behaviour had created a window in which Chinese interest in deepening Nepal engagement was at a temporary peak (Murton, Lord & Beazley, 2016; Adhikari, 2020).

### 3.2 The Himalayan Balancing Model (HBM): An Original Framework

Building on existing small state theory (Walt, 1987; Hey, 2003; Wivel & Thorhallsson, 2018) and resource diplomacy scholarship (Klare, 2012; Shrestha, 2018), this paper proposes a novel "Himalayan Balancing Model" (HBM) as a conceptual framework for understanding Nepal's strategic behaviour. The HBM synthesises four analytical components that together describe the logic and practice of Nepal's BRICS resource diplomacy:

**Component 1 — Resource Niche Identification:** Nepal's recognition of its hydropower endowments as a strategic asset rather than merely a development resource. This shift in framing — from development object to diplomatic instrument — is foundational. It reflects what Wivel and Thorhallsson (2018, p. 7) describe as the small state's capacity to identify "niches in which size-based disadvantages can be converted into leverage."

**Component 2 — Competitive Leveraging:** The deliberate cultivation of competing BRICS member interests in Nepal's resource sectors, creating a competitive dynamic that enhances Nepal's bargaining position. Nepal operationalises competitive leveraging by issuing survey licenses to multiple parties, conducting parallel feasibility studies with different national partners, and maintaining ambiguity about ultimate project award decisions.

**Component 3 — Multilateral Anchoring:** Nepal's strategic use of multilateral frameworks — the NDB, BRI, SAARC, AIIB, and South-South cooperation — to constrain the dominance of any single bilateral partner and create institutional buffers against coercion. This component directly addresses the risk identified by the 2015-2016 blockade: bilateral dependence creates coercive vulnerability, while multilateral anchoring diversifies risk (Keohane, 1969; Hey, 2003).

**Component 4 — Incremental Commitment:** Nepal's practice of making incremental, reversible commitments to multiple BRICS partners, avoiding the lock-in that would foreclose future balancing options. The BRI Framework Agreement of May 2017 — which committed Nepal to the BRI concept without specifying projects, timelines, or financing terms — exemplifies incremental commitment (Adhikari, 2020; Wang, 2019).

The HBM is presented not as a perfectly executed grand strategy but as a pattern of strategic behaviour that emerges from Nepal's structural constraints and opportunities. Individual Nepalese governments may not articulate the HBM as such; nonetheless, the empirical record from 2015 to 2025 reveals its four components as recurrent features of Nepalese resource diplomacy.

### 3.3 Multinational Enterprise Theoretical Lens

From an MNE and International Business perspective, Nepal's resource sector engagement by BRICS-affiliated entities reflects Dunning's (1988) eclectic paradigm of foreign direct investment, widely known as the OLI framework. BRICS-affiliated state-owned enterprises investing in Nepal's hydropower sector demonstrate classic OLI advantages: technological expertise in large-scale dam construction and project finance (Ownership advantage); Nepal's unique resource endowments, growing energy market, and geopolitical positioning (Location advantage); and the strategic value of controlling regional energy supply chains (Internalisation advantage) (Dunning & Lundan, 2008).

However, BRICS SOEs also operate under distinctive political economy logics that diverge from conventional MNE theory. As Cuervo-Cazurra et al. (2014, p. 920) observe, state-owned multinationals "respond to institutional logics that extend beyond commercial profit maximisation to include state strategic objectives, including energy security, geopolitical influence, and diplomatic positioning." Leutert (2019, p. 29) similarly argues that Chinese SOEs operating abroad "maintain institutional linkages with the state apparatus that shape their investment priorities in ways that pure commercial logic cannot explain." This political-commercial hybrid logic makes standard MNE analytical frameworks partially applicable to the Nepal case but requires supplementation with international relations theory — the synthesis this paper undertakes.

## 4. Analysis

### 4.1 India's Engagement: Hegemonic Presence and Hydropower Competition

India constitutes Nepal's most consequential BRICS partner, reflecting deep historical, cultural, linguistic, and economic ties. India accounts for approximately 65% of Nepal's total trade, serves as Nepal's primary energy export market, and maintains an open border with Nepal that facilitates the movement of approximately 3-4 million Nepalese workers to India annually (Government of Nepal, Ministry of Finance, 2023; Muni, 2012). This relationship of deep economic interdependency simultaneously provides Nepal with development opportunities and exposes it to structural vulnerability that has periodically been exploited through coercive diplomatic pressure (Baral, 2012).

#### 4.1.1 The Arun-3 Hydropower Project: Competitive Leveraging in Practice

India's flagship hydropower engagement in Nepal is the Arun-3 Hydroelectric Project (900 MW), developed by SJVN Limited — a joint venture of the Government of India and the Government of Himachal Pradesh. The Arun-3 project, situated in the Sankhuwasabha district of eastern Nepal on the Arun River, was awarded to SJVN through a survey licence in 2008, but the development agreement was only finalised by Nepal's Investment Board in 2018 — a ten-year negotiation period that reflects both the complexity of large-scale hydropower contracting and Nepal's deliberate use of strategic delay to maximise concessions (SJVN Limited, 2022; Bhattarai, 2021).

The final development agreement for Arun-3 exemplifies the conditionality reciprocity mechanism of supplier-side resource diplomacy. Nepal negotiated: (1) a 22% free equity stake for the Government of Nepal in the project company; (2) a 21.9% free power entitlement during the project's 25-year operations period; (3) construction of approximately 500 km of access roads in remote mountain districts; (4) development of a 180 MW distribution system serving local communities; and (5) employment quotas requiring that 80% of project personnel be Nepalese nationals in non-specialised roles (SJVN Limited, 2022). Khadka (2018, p. 21) argues persuasively that these concessions — substantially better than initial Indian offers — reflected "the negotiating leverage Nepal derived from simultaneous Chinese feasibility assessments on adjacent Arun-4 and Arun-5 sites," demonstrating competitive leveraging in operation.

The Power Trade Agreement (PTA) signed between Nepal and India in 2014 — and its subsequent amendment in 2021 to allow third-country power trading — represents a complementary milestone in Nepal's hydropower diplomacy. The 2021 amendment, permitting Nepal to sell electricity through India to Bangladesh, opened a new market dimension that enhanced the commercial viability of Nepal's hydropower sector and reduced its exclusive dependence on the Indian energy market (Nepal Electricity Authority, 2022). From a supplier-side resource diplomacy perspective, the expanded PTA represents a critical success: Nepal converted its geographical disadvantage — dependence on Indian territory for any power export — into a managed interdependency with commercial benefits for both parties.

#### 4.1.2 The Pancheswor Multipurpose Project: The Limits of Bilateral Cooperation

The Pancheswor Multipurpose Project (5,040 MW) — the largest proposed hydropower project in South Asia, to be developed jointly by Nepal and India on the Mahakali River — illustrates both the potential and limitations of Nepal-India hydropower cooperation. Envisaged since the 1996 Mahakali Treaty, Pancheswor has remained in the pre-construction phase for nearly three decades, with fundamental disagreements over benefit sharing, cost allocation, and the interpretation of treaty provisions remaining unresolved as of 2025 (Baral, 2012; Shrestha, 2018). Shrestha (2018, p. 797) characterises Pancheswor as a "case study in hydro-hegemonic stagnation," arguing that India's structural leverage over Nepal's economy allows it to defer implementation of treaty commitments without facing meaningful consequences. The Pancheswor case illustrates the boundaries of Nepal's competitive leveraging strategy: where no credible Chinese alternative exists (the Mahakali is a purely Nepal-India shared river), Nepal's bargaining leverage is structurally limited.

#### 4.1.3 India's Connectivity Investments: Responding to the China Factor

Beyond hydropower, India has made significant investments in Nepal's surface connectivity infrastructure, partly in response to China's BRI commitments. The Integrated Check Posts (ICP) programme — financed by India at key Nepal-India border points including Birgunj, Biratnagar, Bhairahawa, and Nepalgunj — has modernised Nepal's border infrastructure and facilitated expanded bilateral trade flows (Indian Embassy Kathmandu, 2021). The Raxaul-Kathmandu railway project, long promised and repeatedly delayed, gained new momentum from 2020 onward as India sought to counter China's Kerung-Kathmandu railway proposal with its own connectivity narrative (Pant, 2017; Indian Embassy Kathmandu, 2021).

India's EXIM Bank financing for Nepal's postal highway expansion and the Hulaki Highway — connecting remote Terai (southern plains) districts — reflects a broader Indian strategy of using infrastructure financing to consolidate influence in Nepal's southern regions while China focuses on northern Himalayan connectivity (Murton, Lord & Beazley, 2016). This geographic specialisation in India-China infrastructure competition — India in the Terai, China in the mountains — reflects the structural logic of each country's comparative geographic advantages in Nepal engagement.

#### 4.1.4 The 2015-2016 Blockade: A Strategic Inflection Point

No analysis of India's BRICS-context engagement with Nepal can omit the 2015-2016 economic blockade. From September 2015 to February 2016, India effectively restricted the flow of petroleum products and essential goods through Raxaul and other border crossing points, causing acute fuel shortages, power outages, and supply chain disruptions throughout Nepal (Murton, Lord & Beazley, 2016). The blockade's ostensible trigger was Nepal's adoption of its 2015 constitution without sufficient provisions for Madhesi minority communities in the Terai — a constitutional concern that Indian policymakers chose to address through economic coercion rather than diplomatic engagement (Ogura, 2008).

The blockade had two lasting strategic consequences. First, it demonstrated to Nepal's political elite with devastating clarity that bilateral economic dependence on India created coercive vulnerability — a lesson that accelerated Nepal's engagement with the BRI, the AIIB, and other China-anchored multilateral frameworks (Adhikari, 2020). Second, it generated a wave of Nepali nationalist sentiment directed against India that constrained subsequent Nepali governments' ability to make concessions to India in hydropower and trade negotiations — ironically limiting India's ability to exploit the very leverage it had demonstrated. As Thapa (2018, p. 62) observes, "the blockade may have been India's most counterproductive act of coercive diplomacy in the modern era," producing strategic outcomes diametrically opposed to its diplomatic objectives.

### 4.2 China's Engagement: BRI and the New Connectivity Architecture

China's engagement with Nepal has expanded dramatically since the 2015 earthquake and accelerated following Nepal's BRI accession in May 2017. China's strategy in Nepal operates across three primary dimensions: connectivity infrastructure, trade facilitation, and development financing — all of which intersect with Nepal's resource diplomacy agenda.

#### 4.2.1 The Belt and Road Initiative: Projects, Progress, and Pitfalls

Nepal's BRI engagement encompasses nine priority projects across connectivity, energy, and economic corridor development (Nepal Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2023). The flagship project — and the most geopolitically consequential — is the Kerung-Kathmandu Cross-Border Railway. Proposed as a 72-kilometre extension of the existing Lhasa-Shigatse-Kerung railway from the China-Nepal border at Rasuwagadhi to Kathmandu, this project would transform Nepal's strategic position: Nepal would become the first Himalayan state with rail connectivity to China, potentially reducing its transit dependence on India and opening the Tibetan market to Nepalese goods (Wang, 2019; Huang, 2016).

A detailed feasibility report for the Kerung-Kathmandu Railway was submitted by China Railway Fifth Survey and Design Institute Group in 2020, confirming the project's technical feasibility while flagging significant challenges: extreme elevation differentials (the line would traverse terrain from approximately 1,300m above sea level in Kathmandu to 4,540m at the border); seismic risks in Nepal's earthquake zone; and construction costs estimated at USD 2.7–3.2 billion, representing approximately 25–30% of Nepal's GDP at 2020 values (Adhikari, 2020; Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, 2021). The debt sustainability implications of railway financing at market or near-market rates are severe: Nepal would require decades of robust economic growth and railway revenue generation to service such debt without fiscal distress.

As of 2025, the Kerung-Kathmandu Railway remains in the pre-construction phase. Nepal's incremental commitment strategy — exemplified by its willingness to engage with feasibility studies and project planning without committing to specific financing arrangements — has allowed it to capture the diplomatic benefits of BRI participation (Chinese engagement, geopolitical signalling to India) while deferring the financial risks of actual construction. Adhikari (2020, p. 437) describes this approach as "strategic ambiguity," noting that "Nepal has remained in the BRI framework long enough to derive diplomatic benefits without accepting financial commitments that could compromise fiscal sovereignty."

Beyond the railway, China financed and constructed the Pokhara International Airport — Nepal's third international airport, completed in January 2023 at a cost of USD 215.96 million financed through Chinese EXIM Bank loans at 2% interest over 20 years (Nepal Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2023). The airport, intended to facilitate tourist access to the Annapurna and Dhaulagiri mountain regions, has faced substantial controversy: passenger volumes in its first year of operation were significantly below projections, raising questions about commercial viability and the political economy of a Chinese EXIM Bank-financed infrastructure project in a region where strategic rather than commercial logic may have dominated project selection (Bhattarai, 2021).

#### 4.2.2 China's Hydropower Strategy: Competitive Counter-Presence

China's engagement in Nepal's hydropower sector serves both commercial and geopolitical functions, constituting a deliberate counter-presence to Indian hydropower dominance. The Budhi Gandaki Hydropower Project (1,200 MW), one of Nepal's priority storage hydropower projects, was initially allocated to China Gezhouba Group Corporation (CGGC) in 2016 through a government-to-government agreement facilitated by Prime Minister Pushpa Kamal Dahal's government. The project was controversially cancelled in 2018 by the subsequent Sher Bahadur Deuba government — citing procurement irregularities and absence of competitive bidding — before being re-awarded to CGGC following a parliamentary debate and government change (Khadka, 2018; Bhattarai, 2021).

The Budhi Gandaki case is analytically rich for several reasons. First, it illustrates that Nepal's domestic political system introduces significant complexity into its resource diplomacy: different governments have maintained different preferences regarding the India-China balance in hydropower investment, reflecting the party-wise alignment noted above. Second, the cancellation-and-reinstatement sequence demonstrated that domestic political opposition to Chinese projects can force renegotiation — which in the Budhi Gandaki case resulted in improved project terms for Nepal, including enhanced free power provisions (Bhattarai, 2021). Third, Indian concern about Chinese control of the Budhi Gandaki — a major tributary of the Narayani/Gandaki, which crosses the Nepal-India border and carries strategic water security implications — illustrates the security dimensions of hydropower diplomacy that extend beyond commercial considerations (Shrestha, 2018; Chellaney, 2011).

China's PowerChina's West Seti Hydropower Project (750 MW) and CGGC's Upper Marsyangdi-2 (600 MW) further extend Chinese presence in Nepal's hydropower sector. These projects, collectively representing over 2,550 MW of potential capacity, constitute a significant competitive counter to Indian hydropower dominance and represent Nepal's most tangible success in applying competitive leveraging dynamics to its hydropower sector (Nepal Water and Energy Development Company, 2022). The combined pipeline of Indian- and Chinese-affiliated hydropower projects under development in Nepal by 2023 — exceeding 5,000 MW — represents a dramatic acceleration from the negligible foreign investment in the sector before 2010, validating the competitive leveraging dynamics the HBM predicts.

#### 4.2.3 Trade Connectivity and the Chinese Seaport Access Agreement

China has progressively expanded trade access points on the Nepal-Tibet border, with Rasuwagadhi, Tatopani (reopened after 2015 earthquake damage), and Olangchung Gola customs points providing multiple routes for Chinese goods and Nepalese exports. More strategically significant is the Trade and Transit Agreement signed between Nepal and China in 2016, which established Nepal's right to use Chinese seaports at Tianjin, Shenzhen, Lianyungang, and Zhanjiang for third-country trade (Murton, Lord & Beazley, 2016). This agreement theoretically liberates Nepal from complete dependence on the Indian port of Kolkata — the exclusive gateway for Nepal's third-country imports since 1950 — and represents a fundamental transformation in Nepal's transit geography, even if logistical and cost constraints have limited actual utilisation of Chinese seaports to date.

Murton, Lord, and Beazley (2016, p. 428) characterise the 2016 Trade and Transit Agreement as "a geopolitical milestone of the first order, regardless of its immediate practical impact," arguing that the mere existence of a Chinese seaport alternative "permanently alters the coercive calculus India can bring to bear in bilateral disputes." The 2015-2016 blockade's effectiveness depended on Nepal having no alternative transit route; the 2016 agreement, even if operationally limited, reduces this vulnerability meaningfully.

### 4.3 The New Development Bank: Multilateral Financing Architecture

The New Development Bank (NDB), established by BRICS members in 2015 with initial authorised capital of USD 100 billion, represents a qualitatively different dimension of Nepal's potential BRICS engagement — one involving multilateral rather than bilateral financing architecture. Nepal is not a founding NDB member, but its growing interest in NDB financing reflects the institution's strategic positioning as an alternative to traditional multilateral development banks (New Development Bank, 2024; Humphrey, 2015).

#### 4.3.1 NDB's Institutional Character and Relevance for Nepal

The NDB's lending philosophy differs from both the World Bank and bilateral Chinese EXIM Bank financing in ways that are potentially advantageous for Nepal. Unlike the World Bank, the NDB does not impose governance conditionalities as standard lending requirements — a feature that appeals to borrowers sceptical of Western-defined governance benchmarks (Humphrey, 2015). Unlike Chinese bilateral EXIM Bank loans, NDB financing is multilaterally governed and benefits from the institutional oversight of five member-state shareholders with diverse interests — a feature that reduces the bilateral political leverage any single member can exercise over borrowing countries (New Development Bank, 2024; Stuenkel, 2020).

By 2024, the NDB had expanded its membership beyond the original BRICS five to include Bangladesh, Egypt, UAE, Uruguay, and Ethiopia, with commitments exceeding USD 30 billion across 96 projects (New Development Bank, 2024). The NDB's clean energy mandate — which prioritises renewable energy, energy efficiency, and green infrastructure — aligns closely with Nepal's hydropower development priorities, potentially making the NDB a significant financing partner for medium-scale hydropower projects that fall below the threshold of bilateral government-to-government arrangements.

#### 4.3.2 Nepal's NDB Engagement: Current Status and Prospects

Nepal has engaged with NDB mechanisms through observer status discussions and co-financing conversations, but has not yet secured full membership or concluded specific lending agreements as of 2025. Nepal's path to NDB membership is complicated by China-India tensions within the NDB board — both countries have interests in shaping Nepal's development financing landscape, and NDB membership for Nepal could shift the balance of financing influence in ways that neither fully endorses. Nonetheless, the NDB's expansion trajectory and its geographic focus on Asia suggest that Nepal's eventual membership is plausible within the medium term (Stuenkel, 2020; New Development Bank, 2024).

From a small state strategy perspective, NDB membership would represent a significant multilateral anchoring achievement for Nepal: it would provide an additional financing option with BRICS legitimacy but without the bilateral political entanglements of Indian or Chinese direct investment, and would create an institutional forum in which Nepal could engage BRICS members collectively rather than exclusively bilaterally (Humphrey, 2015; Hey, 2003).

### 4.4 The Broader BRICS Ecosystem: Brazil, Russia, and South Africa

While India and China dominate Nepal's BRICS engagement, the other three original BRICS members — Brazil, Russia, and South Africa — play secondary but analytically significant roles in Nepal's resource diplomacy context.

**Brazil's Contributions:** Brazil's most significant contribution to Nepal's development trajectory comes through its technical expertise in large-scale hydropower development and management. Brazilian engineering models, particularly those developed for Itaipu (14,000 MW, world's second-largest hydropower plant) and Belo Monte (11,233 MW), have informed Nepal's thinking about storage hydropower project management and indigenous institutional capacity building (Nepal Water and Energy Development Company, 2022). Brazil's engagement in Nepal's hydropower sector — while not involving direct investment — demonstrates that South-South knowledge transfer occurs within BRICS frameworks through channels other than direct financing. Brazil's Embraer commercial aircraft presence in Nepal Airlines Corporation's fleet reflects modest but real commercial engagement (Nepal Airlines Corporation, 2020).

**Russia's Diplomatic Role:** Russia's engagement with Nepal has been significant in the political-diplomatic dimension, particularly through its UN Security Council position. Russia has consistently supported positions in the Security Council that favour sovereignty-respecting approaches to international development — a stance that Nepal, navigating between two giant neighbours, values as an institutional check on great power coercion (Thapa, 2018). Russia has also engaged Nepal through military equipment supply, educational scholarships, and tourism promotion, maintaining a low-intensity bilateral relationship that contributes to Nepal's multilateral anchoring strategy by ensuring that Nepal has some engagement with all five original BRICS members. Russia's supply of Mi-17 military helicopters to the Nepal Army and Nepalese student placements in Russian universities represent tangible if modest dimensions of this engagement (Ogura, 2008; Thapa, 2018).

**South Africa's Normative Influence:** South Africa's engagement with Nepal is primarily channelled through UN peacekeeping coordination — Nepal is one of the world's largest per-capita contributors to UN peacekeeping operations, deploying approximately 6,000 troops annually — and through Commonwealth-adjacent development cooperation frameworks (Upreti, 2016). South Africa's technical assistance in governance reform and anti-corruption has supplemented Nepal's post-2015 federal governance restructuring, though the direct impact on resource diplomacy is indirect.

### 4.5 Nepal's Strategic Responses: Four Empirical Patterns

Nepal's strategic responses to BRICS member competition can be organised around four empirically observable patterns that collectively operationalise the Himalayan Balancing Model.

#### 4.5.1 Competitive Licensing and Re-Licensing

Nepal's Investment Board Nepal (IBN) has demonstrated a recurrent pattern of using the prospect of alternative BRICS investor engagement to renegotiate project terms with existing licence holders. The Upper Karnali Hydropower Project (900 MW) provides the paradigmatic example: initially licensed to GMR Group of India in 2008, the project underwent repeated renegotiation between 2012 and 2020, with Nepal's IBN using documented Chinese interest in the Karnali basin — where Chinese entities had conducted independent feasibility surveys — to extract improved terms from GMR including enhanced free power provisions (increased from 12% to 21.9% following 2018 renegotiation), expanded road connectivity commitments, and Nepali employment quotas (Khadka, 2018; Bhattarai, 2021).

This competitive licensing pattern is not unique to the Karnali. Bhattarai (2021, p. 11) documents that "across Nepal's hydropower licensing pipeline from 2010 to 2020, the presence of Chinese expressions of interest — even when not resulting in formal bids — demonstrably improved the terms extracted from Indian and third-country investors in subsequent negotiations." This pattern is the clearest empirical evidence for the competitive leveraging mechanism at the heart of the HBM.

#### 4.5.2 Multilateral Institutional Anchoring

Nepal has strategically joined multiple multilateral frameworks to create institutional buffers against bilateral dominance. Nepal's accession to the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB) in 2016 — of which China is the largest shareholder at approximately 26.6% — provides access to Chinese-led multilateral financing with governance standards more transparent than bilateral Chinese EXIM Bank loans, including independent project evaluation and environmental and social safeguard requirements comparable to World Bank standards (Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, 2021).

The AIIB case illustrates the multilateral anchoring mechanism: Nepal accesses Chinese-affiliated financing without the bilateral political entanglements of direct Chinese government loans, while the AIIB's multilateral governance provides some institutional protection against the leverage asymmetries that characterise bilateral Nepal-China financing arrangements. As the AIIB (2021, p. 14) notes, "governance standards at the AIIB are explicitly designed to reduce the bilateralisation of development financing," a design feature that serves Nepal's diversification objectives.

Nepal's simultaneous maintenance of active membership in SAARC (where India is institutionally dominant), BIMSTEC, the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (as a dialogue partner), and the South Asian University — all institutions with different principal-power profiles — reflects the institutional diversification logic of multilateral anchoring (Hey, 2003; Thapa, 2018).

#### 4.5.3 Sequenced Bilateral Commitments

Nepal's practice of signing framework agreements and memoranda of understanding with multiple BRICS members on overlapping issue areas — without converting all agreements into binding legal commitments simultaneously — preserves strategic flexibility. The BRI Framework Agreement of May 2017, signed under Prime Minister Deuba, committed Nepal to the BRI concept without specifying projects, timelines, or financing terms (Adhikari, 2020; Wang, 2019). This strategic ambiguity — committing to the framework while deferring to project specifics — allowed Nepal to signal engagement to China while maintaining room for renegotiation on individual project terms.

Subsequent Nepalese governments have interpreted BRI commitments variably: the KP Sharma Oli government (2018–2021) prioritised BRI implementation and promoted the Kerung-Kathmandu Railway actively, while the Sher Bahadur Deuba government (2021–2022) was more cautious, emphasising grant financing over loans and questioning the debt sustainability of railway construction (Nepal Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2023). This variation in commitment across government changes reflects the incremental commitment mechanism of the HBM — preserving optionality by never making binding commitments that foreclose future balancing moves.

#### 4.5.4 Domestic Political Coalition Management

Nepal's federal democratic political system introduces a domestic political layer that pure IR theory tends to underestimate in small state strategy. Different political parties maintain differential alignments: the Nepali Congress (NC) has historically been more comfortable with India engagement, while the successive communist parties — CPN-UML, CPN (Maoist Centre), and their unified and re-split variants — have tended toward closer China engagement (Ogura, 2008; Thapa, 2018). Government changes in Nepal — which have been frequent, with 13 distinct governments between 2006 and 2023 — have periodically resulted in shifts in hydropower project priorities, renegotiations of Chinese commitments, and recalibrations of Indian engagement.

This domestic political variability, while creating implementation challenges that frustrate foreign partners, also provides Nepal's governments with plausible domestic political cover for strategic renegotiations: a new government can attribute project renegotiations to domestic political necessity rather than deliberate strategic manoeuvre, preserving the diplomatic relationship while securing improved terms. Bhattarai (2021, p. 15) describes this as "domestic politics as diplomatic shield," observing that Nepal's "frequent government changes have paradoxically enhanced rather than diminished its negotiating flexibility" by providing regular opportunities to revisit commitments.

### 4.6 Development Outcomes: Achievements and Structural Risks

#### 4.6.1 Positive Development Outcomes: Hydropower and Connectivity

Nepal's competitive engagement with BRICS resource diplomacy has generated measurable positive development outcomes across multiple sectors. Installed hydropower capacity grew from approximately 800 MW in 2015 to over 2,400 MW by 2023 — a three-fold increase over eight years, representing the most rapid capacity expansion in Nepal's history (Nepal Electricity Authority, 2023). This expansion has transformed Nepal from a chronic power-deficit country — which experienced up to 18 hours of daily load shedding in 2015-2016 — into a power-surplus country by 2022, with surplus electricity available for export (Nepal Electricity Authority, 2022).

The Upper Tamakoshi Hydroelectric Project (456 MW), while primarily financed domestically through the Employees Provident Fund, Citizens Investment Trust, and public share issuance, benefited from Chinese construction expertise (primary civil contractor: China Gezhouba Group Corporation) and demonstrated the productive hybridisation possible when domestic financial ownership is combined with BRICS-affiliated technical capacity (Nepal Water and Energy Development Company, 2022). The project's successful completion in 2021, on budget and ahead of schedule, represented a landmark in Nepal's domestic hydropower development capacity.

Road connectivity in Nepal's northern Himalayan districts has improved substantially through BRI-linked and Chinese grant financing, with the Araniko Highway rehabilitation, the Hilsa-Simikot road, and multiple district road projects providing market access for mountain communities that had been effectively isolated from the national economy (Adhikari, 2020; Murton, Lord & Beazley, 2016). India's simultaneous Terai road programmes have similarly improved connectivity in the southern plains. The combined effect of BRICS-affiliated north-south infrastructure investment represents a significant improvement in national economic integration, even if the geopolitical motivations of investors are distinct from Nepal's development priorities.

#### 4.6.2 Structural Risks: Debt, Dependency, and Environmental Vulnerability

Significant structural risks accompany Nepal's BRICS resource diplomacy engagement. Nepal's external debt stock reached approximately NPR 1.2 trillion (approximately USD 9 billion) by 2023, representing a dramatic increase from NPR 327 billion (USD 2.9 billion) in 2015 — a trebling in eight years (Government of Nepal, Ministry of Finance, 2023). The composition of this debt has shifted from predominantly concessional multilateral lending (ADB, World Bank) toward a greater proportion of market-rate commercial borrowing, including Chinese EXIM Bank loans for infrastructure projects (Government of Nepal, Ministry of Finance, 2023; Humphrey, 2015).

The debt sustainability concern is illustrated most vividly by the Pokhara Airport case. Financed by a USD 215.96 million Chinese EXIM Bank loan at 2% interest over 20 years, Pokhara Airport handled approximately 450,000 passengers in its first year of operation — approximately 30% of its projected capacity — generating insufficient revenue to service the debt independently (Nepal Ministry of Foreign Affairs, 2023). Nepal's broader tourism-linked infrastructure investments expose it to concentration risk: if tourism revenues underperform projections — as occurred in 2020-2022 due to COVID-19 — debt service obligations become severe fiscal stresses (Government of Nepal, Ministry of Finance, 2023).

Asymmetric power relations constitute a second structural vulnerability that the HBM can mitigate but not eliminate. Despite Nepal's competitive leveraging strategy, the fundamental power asymmetry between Nepal and its BRICS neighbours means that Nepal's bargaining leverage operates at the margins: it can improve project terms within the range that India and China are willing to accept, but cannot fundamentally alter the strategic logic that drives their Nepal engagement. As Shrestha (2018, p. 800) observes, "Nepal's competitive leveraging success has been real but bounded — it has improved the terms of dependence without fundamentally reducing dependence itself."

Environmental and social risks from large-scale hydropower development represent a third structural vulnerability. Several proposed hydropower projects — including Budhi Gandaki and Pancheswor — involve significant population displacement (Budhi Gandaki alone would displace an estimated 10,000 people), seismic risks given Nepal's earthquake-prone geology, and downstream environmental impacts on river ecosystems (International Rivers, 2020; Shrestha, 2018). International Rivers (2020, p. 12) documents that "governance of environmental impact assessments for BRI-linked hydropower projects in Nepal has been less rigorous than for World Bank-financed equivalents," raising concerns about long-term environmental sustainability that could become significant political liabilities.

## 5. Research Contributions and Remaining Gaps

### 5.1 Contributions of This Paper

This paper makes four distinct contributions to existing scholarship:

**First,** it establishes Nepal as a substantive case study in BRICS scholarship, addressing the near-total absence of Nepal-focused analysis in a literature dominated by African and Latin American cases. Nepal's unique geographic position between two BRICS giants provides an analytically distinctive vantage point that generates insights not replicable from other case studies. Specifically, Nepal is one of the few countries where two BRICS members are in direct territorial adjacency and actively competing for influence — a structural condition that maximises the competitive leveraging dynamics the HBM describes.

**Second,** it develops the concept of "supplier-side resource diplomacy" — the idea that resource-holding developing states can employ their natural assets as active diplomatic instruments rather than merely receiving the attentions of resource-seeking great powers. This concept extends and enriches the existing resource diplomacy literature (Klare, 2012; Shrestha, 2018), which has been predominantly concerned with resource-seeking strategies of importing states. The three mechanisms of supplier-side resource diplomacy — competitive attraction, conditionality reciprocity, and strategic sequencing — provide an analytically tractable vocabulary for empirical research on other resource-holding small states.

**Third,** the Himalayan Balancing Model (HBM) contributes a novel theoretical framework for understanding small state agency in multipolar geopolitical environments. The HBM's four components — resource niche identification, competitive leveraging, multilateral anchoring, and incremental commitment — extend existing small state theory (Walt, 1987; Hey, 2003; Wivel & Thorhallsson, 2018) by incorporating the resource diplomacy dimension and situating it within the specific dynamics of Himalayan geopolitics. The HBM has potential applicability beyond Nepal to other small, resource-rich states navigating great power competition, including Bhutan (in hydropower), Tajikistan (in water and minerals), and Cambodia (in strategic location and hydropower).

**Fourth,** it integrates MNE/International Business theory (Dunning, 1988; Dunning & Lundan, 2008; Cuervo-Cazurra et al., 2014) with international relations scholarship on small state strategy to analyse BRICS-affiliated SOEs in Nepal's resource sector. This theoretical integration is underdeveloped in both IB literature — which tends to treat MNEs as primarily commercial actors — and IR literature — which tends to treat states as monolithic strategic actors — and the Nepal case provides rich empirical material for advancing this synthesis. Specifically, the paper demonstrates how Dunning's OLI framework applies to SOE-mediated resource diplomacy when supplemented with Leutert's (2019) analysis of state enterprise political-commercial hybridity.

### 5.2 Remaining Research Gaps and Future Agenda

Despite this paper's contributions, significant research gaps remain. **Quantitative impact assessment** of BRICS-affiliated investments on Nepal's development indicators — GDP growth, energy access rates, trade volumes, poverty rates — requires systematic econometric analysis beyond this paper's qualitative scope. Future research should employ difference-in-differences or synthetic control methods to isolate the development effects of BRICS investment from other drivers, and to compare Nepal's trajectory with comparable small states that adopted different engagement strategies.

**Sub-national and distributional dimensions** of Nepal's resource diplomacy — particularly the differential impacts on mountain, hill, and Terai communities; on ethnic and caste groups; and on gender equity — remain largely unexamined. The distribution of development benefits from hydropower projects raises important social equity questions: who bears the costs of dam construction (typically displaced rural communities) versus who benefits from electricity access (often urban consumers and industrial users)? These distributional questions are invisible in national-level analyses.

**The post-2024 BRICS expansion** and its implications for Nepal deserve dedicated scholarly attention. The addition of Saudi Arabia, UAE, Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, and Argentina — all with distinct interests in renewable energy, connectivity infrastructure, and South Asian markets — creates new potential dimensions in Nepal's resource diplomacy options that this paper cannot fully address. Saudi and UAE sovereign wealth fund interest in clean energy investment, in particular, could create new financing options for Nepal's hydropower sector.

**Comparative analysis** of Nepal's strategy relative to other small Himalayan and landlocked states — Bhutan (deep India alignment, controlled hydropower export), Sri Lanka (Chinese debt distress, now recovering), Laos (BRI-linked railway, debt sustainability concerns), and Kyrgyzstan (hydropower with Russia and China engagement) — would enrich understanding of the HBM's applicability and limitations across diverse geopolitical contexts.

## 6. Conclusion and Policy Implications

### 6.1 Summary of Findings

This paper has examined Nepal's strategic navigation of BRICS resource diplomacy across 2015–2025, revealing a country that has evolved from relative passivity in international economic diplomacy to active, if structurally constrained, strategic agency. Nepal's hydropower resources — vast but as yet largely undeveloped, with 83,000 MW of potential against less than 2,500 MW harnessed — have emerged as the primary currency of its BRICS resource diplomacy, attracting competing Indian, Chinese, and broader BRICS-affiliated investments and creating bargaining leverage that Nepal has increasingly learned to deploy (International Energy Agency, 2021; Nepal Electricity Authority, 2023).

The analysis, organised through the Himalayan Balancing Model, demonstrates that Nepal has pursued a coherent, if not always explicitly articulated, competitive leveraging strategy across four mechanisms: cultivating competing interests, anchoring engagements in multilateral frameworks, maintaining strategic ambiguity in commitments, and managing domestic political coalitions as diplomatic resources. This strategy has generated measurable development gains — particularly in installed hydropower capacity (800 MW to 2,400 MW, 2015–2023), northern connectivity, border trade infrastructure, and market access — while also exposing Nepal to structural vulnerabilities in debt sustainability, environmental governance, and asymmetric power dependence (Nepal Electricity Authority, 2023; Government of Nepal, Ministry of Finance, 2023).

The broader theoretical argument — that small, resource-endowed states can exercise meaningful strategic agency through supplier-side resource diplomacy — finds empirical support in the Nepal case, while the structural constraints documented throughout the analysis counsel against overstating this agency. Nepal's competitive leveraging strategy has improved the terms of its resource diplomacy arrangements rather than fundamentally altering the power asymmetries it navigates. The HBM describes a strategy for marginal improvement within structural constraints, not for escaping those constraints.

### 6.2 Policy Implications for Nepalese Policymakers

**Recommendation 1 — Institutionalise Resource Diplomacy Coordination:** Nepal should establish a dedicated Resource Diplomacy Coordination Unit within the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, tasked with coordinating hydropower licensing, financing negotiations, and project monitoring across all BRICS partners in a coherent strategic framework. Currently, this coordination is fragmented across the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Ministry of Energy, Ministry of Finance, and the Investment Board Nepal, with each agency maintaining separate bilateral relationships that reduce Nepal's negotiating coherence and enable divide-and-rule dynamics by major powers (Thapa, 2018; Government of Nepal, Ministry of Finance, 2023).

**Recommendation 2 — Strengthen Debt Sustainability Frameworks:** Nepal should adopt a formal Debt Sustainability Analysis (DSA) requirement for all project financing exceeding USD 50 million, with independent review mechanisms. This would provide early warning of unsustainable debt accumulation from market-rate BRICS financing, particularly Chinese EXIM Bank loans, and would create a domestic institutional check on politically motivated project selection that prioritises diplomatic signalling over commercial viability (Humphrey, 2015; Government of Nepal, Ministry of Finance, 2023).

**Recommendation 3 — Diversify Energy Export Markets:** Nepal should actively operationalise the third-country electricity trading provisions of the amended 2021 Power Trade Agreement, expanding power sales to Bangladesh and pursuing similar arrangements with additional South Asian markets. Multilateral energy trading frameworks within SAARC or BIMSTEC architecture would enhance Nepal's resource diplomacy leverage by expanding the market for its primary strategic asset and reducing Indian monopsony power in Nepal's electricity sector (Nepal Electricity Authority, 2022; Bhattarai, 2021).

**Recommendation 4 — Pursue NDB Membership:** Nepal should actively pursue full NDB membership or associate member status, which would provide access to multilateral development financing with BRICS legitimacy but without the bilateral political entanglements of Indian or Chinese direct financing. NDB membership would also create an additional multilateral forum for Nepal-BRICS engagement, supplementing the existing bilateral and regional frameworks (Humphrey, 2015; New Development Bank, 2024; Stuenkel, 2020).

**Recommendation 5 — Mandatory Seismic Safety Standards:** Nepal should establish and enforce international best-practice seismic safety standards for all BRICS-affiliated hydropower projects as a non-negotiable licensing condition, administered by an independent regulatory body with genuine enforcement capacity. Given Nepal's acute seismic vulnerability — demonstrated by the 2015 Gorkha earthquake — this requirement serves both public safety and long-term project viability objectives, and would differentiate Nepal's regulatory environment favourably from less demanding alternatives in the regional hydropower market (International Rivers, 2020; Shrestha, 2018).

### 6.3 Policy Implications for MNEs and International Investors

Nepal's BRICS resource diplomacy engagement creates both opportunities and risks for international commercial actors. The competitive dynamic between Indian and Chinese state-owned enterprises creates space for third-country private sector participation in hydropower and infrastructure — particularly in project management, environmental services, hydroelectric equipment manufacturing, and digital infrastructure — where neither Indian nor Chinese firms hold decisive advantages. MNEs with expertise in high-altitude construction, hydroelectric turbine manufacturing, dam safety assessment, and environmental impact management should view Nepal's growing hydropower sector as a commercially attractive niche market with demand driven by both state-affiliated and independent power producer development (Dunning & Lundan, 2008; Cuervo-Cazurra et al., 2014).

However, political risk management is essential for all international commercial actors in Nepal. Nepal's domestic political volatility — including frequent government changes, parliamentary debates over project allocations, and periodic nationalist mobilisations — creates implementation uncertainty that must be priced into project viability assessments. Political risk insurance through multilateral guarantors — MIGA (Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency), ADB credit guarantee facilities, and the AIIB's project preparation fund — should be considered standard practice for large-scale Nepal resource sector investments (Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, 2021; Thacker, 2022).

### 6.4 Broader Lessons for Small State Resource Diplomacy

Nepal's experience between 2015 and 2025 offers transferable lessons for other small, resource-endowed states navigating great power competition. The core insight is that resource endowments — even when largely undeveloped — can function as effective diplomatic instruments when a state possesses sufficient institutional capacity to manage competing interests strategically. The prerequisites for successful supplier-side resource diplomacy are: (1) coherent domestic governance of resource licensing and project management; (2) multilateral institutional anchoring to constrain bilateral dominance; (3) sequential commitment strategies that preserve future flexibility; (4) domestic political coalition management to sustain strategic consistency across government changes; and (5) robust debt sustainability frameworks to prevent resource assets from being converted into financial liabilities.

States that have adopted analogous strategies — Rwanda in digital infrastructure investment, Botswana in diamond sector governance, and Bolivia in lithium resource diplomacy — suggest that the supplier-side resource diplomacy concept has application beyond the Nepal-BRICS context, though each case is shaped by its distinct structural conditions (Klare, 2012; Ross, 2012).

### 6.5 Concluding Reflections

Nepal's trajectory between 2015 and 2025 demonstrates that small states are not merely passive objects of great power competition but active, if constrained, agents in the geopolitical processes that shape their development trajectories. The BRICS framework — with its inherent inter-member tensions, particularly between India and China — provides structural opportunities for small states to extract development gains that would be unavailable in a unipolar geopolitical environment. Nepal's strategic navigation of this complex landscape, with its recorded successes, documented failures, and ongoing negotiations, constitutes a significant case study for scholars of international development, international business, and small state strategic behaviour.

As BRICS continues to expand and evolve — potentially incorporating new energy producers and emerging economies beyond the 2024 expansion — Nepal's position at the nexus of Asian connectivity and Himalayan resource wealth will only grow in strategic significance. The challenge for Nepalese policymakers is to translate this geopolitical centrality into sustainable development outcomes that benefit citizens across all regions, communities, and social strata. Resource diplomacy, while a powerful tool in Nepal's strategic arsenal, is ultimately a means to an end: the construction of a prosperous, equitable, and resilient Nepalese society. Ensuring that BRICS resource diplomacy serves this ultimate objective — rather than becoming a vehicle for elite capture, fiscal mismanagement, or environmental degradation — remains the most important policy challenge facing Nepal in the decade ahead.
