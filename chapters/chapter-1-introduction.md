# Chapter 1: Introduction

## A Philosophical Analysis of Dukkha in Modernity: Examining the Relevance of Buddhist Dissatisfaction to the Contemporary Human Condition

---

## 1.1 Background of the Study

The concept of *dukkha* (Pāli: दुक्ख; Sanskrit: duḥkha) stands as one of the most philosophically profound and existentially consequential doctrines in the entire Buddhist intellectual tradition. Proclaimed by Siddhārtha Gautama in his first discourse at Isipatana (the *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta*, SN 56.11), dukkha constitutes the First Noble Truth (*dukkha ariya sacca*) — the foundational diagnostic upon which the entire edifice of Buddhist philosophy, ethics, and soteriology is constructed. Yet dukkha is far more than a statement about pain or suffering in any ordinary sense. As Walpola Rahula (1959) insisted, to translate dukkha simply as "suffering" is both misleading and philosophically impoverished; the term encompasses "imperfection, impermanence, emptiness, and insubstantiality" (p. 17). It is, in its deepest philosophical register, an ontological characterisation of the very nature of conditioned existence (*saṅkhāra*).

The contemporary world — characterised by unprecedented technological advancement, global interconnectedness, material abundance in much of the industrialised sphere, and the proliferation of choices and freedoms — would appear, on the surface, to represent a decisive departure from the conditions that gave rise to the Buddha's diagnosis twenty-five centuries ago. Yet modernity presents a paradox that demands philosophical scrutiny: despite extraordinary material progress, contemporary societies exhibit pervasive and deepening forms of dissatisfaction, existential anxiety, meaninglessness, and what Byung-Chul Han (2015) has termed the pathologies of the "achievement society." The World Health Organisation has declared depression a leading cause of disability worldwide; rates of anxiety, burnout, and what sociologists call "anomie" have reached levels that suggest something structurally embedded in the modern condition itself, rather than merely contingent misfortune.

This paradox — material abundance coexisting with deepening dissatisfaction — is not merely a sociological observation. It is a *philosophical* problem that calls for philosophical analysis. It is the central contention of this thesis that the Buddhist concept of dukkha, far from being an archaic or culturally bounded doctrine, provides a remarkably precise and philosophically rigorous framework for diagnosing the structural conditions of dissatisfaction in contemporary modernity.

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem

The modern world faces what may be described as a crisis of satisfaction. Despite the Enlightenment promise that reason, science, and material progress would liberate humanity from suffering, contemporary societies are marked by escalating rates of psychological distress, a pervasive sense of meaninglessness, and what Zygmunt Bauman (2000) has characterised as the radical instability and impermanence of "liquid modernity." The conditions that were supposed to eliminate suffering have, in significant respects, generated new and more insidious forms of it.

The philosophical problem this thesis addresses is twofold:

1. **The diagnostic problem:** Can the Buddhist philosophical analysis of dukkha — particularly the canonical three-fold typology of dukkha-dukkha, vipariṇāma-dukkha, and saṅkhāra-dukkha — serve as an adequate philosophical framework for understanding the structural conditions of dissatisfaction in contemporary modernity?

2. **The causal-analytic problem:** Can the Buddhist causal framework of *paṭiccasamuppāda* (dependent origination) and its analysis of *taṇhā* (craving) illuminate the mechanisms by which modern social, economic, and cultural structures perpetuate cycles of dissatisfaction?

These questions have not been systematically addressed in the existing literature. While scholars such as David Loy (1996, 2008, 2015) have made important contributions connecting Buddhist thought to modern societal critique, and while a growing body of work in "Buddhist modernism" (McMahan, 2008) has examined how Buddhist concepts have been reinterpreted in the modern era, there remains a significant gap: no sustained, systematic philosophical analysis has deployed the canonical Buddhist typology of dukkha as a diagnostic framework for the structural conditions of modernity. Most contemporary engagement with dukkha in the modern context has been either **psychological** (reducing dukkha to a mental health category), **therapeutic** (focusing on mindfulness-based interventions), or **popular** (presenting Buddhism as a lifestyle or wellness practice). The distinctly *philosophical* dimension — dukkha as an ontological and phenomenological analysis of the nature of conditioned existence — has been insufficiently brought to bear on the question of modernity.

## 1.3 Thesis Statement

This thesis argues that the Buddhist philosophical concept of dukkha, understood in its full canonical depth as encompassing three dimensions — *dukkha-dukkha* (the suffering of suffering), *vipariṇāma-dukkha* (the suffering inherent in impermanence and change), and *saṅkhāra-dukkha* (the suffering inherent in the very nature of conditioned existence) — provides a philosophically rigorous and diagnostically powerful framework for analysing the structural conditions of dissatisfaction in contemporary modernity. Further, it argues that the Buddhist causal analysis through *paṭiccasamuppāda* (dependent origination) and *taṇhā* (craving) illuminates the mechanisms by which modern institutional structures — particularly consumerism, digital culture, and the achievement-performance paradigm — perpetuate and intensify cycles of dissatisfaction in ways that are structurally analogous to the processes described in classical Buddhist philosophy.

The thesis does not claim that the Buddha anticipated modernity, nor that Buddhist philosophy offers a complete social theory. Rather, it demonstrates that the *philosophical categories* developed within the Buddhist tradition possess an analytical precision and diagnostic depth that makes them genuinely illuminating when applied to contemporary phenomena — and that this application reveals dimensions of the modern condition that Western philosophical frameworks alone have not adequately captured.

## 1.4 Research Objectives

The primary objectives of this research are:

1. **To elucidate the philosophical concept of dukkha** in its canonical depth, drawing on the Pāli Canon (particularly the *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta*, SN 56.11), the Abhidhamma, the *Visuddhimagga* of Buddhaghosa, and the Mādhyamika philosophy of Nāgārjuna, with particular attention to the three-fold typology and its philosophical implications.

2. **To characterise the structural conditions of dissatisfaction in contemporary modernity**, drawing on critical social philosophy (Bauman, Han, Rosa) and empirical indicators, to establish the phenomena that require philosophical diagnosis.

3. **To systematically apply the three-fold typology of dukkha** as a philosophical diagnostic framework to the conditions of modernity, demonstrating its analytical adequacy and illuminating power.

4. **To analyse the causal mechanisms of modern dissatisfaction** through the Buddhist philosophical framework of *paṭiccasamuppāda* and *taṇhā*, mapping the three forms of craving (*kāma-taṇhā*, *bhava-taṇhā*, *vibhava-taṇhā*) to specific features of contemporary consumer, digital, and achievement culture.

5. **To evaluate the philosophical implications** of this analysis for both Buddhist philosophy (its relevance and adaptability) and the philosophy of modernity (what Buddhist categories reveal that Western frameworks may miss).

## 1.5 Research Questions

The thesis is guided by the following research questions:

**Primary Research Question:**
> How can the Buddhist philosophical concept of dukkha serve as a diagnostic framework for understanding the structural conditions of dissatisfaction in contemporary modernity?

**Secondary Research Questions:**

1. What are the philosophical dimensions and typological distinctions within the canonical Buddhist analysis of dukkha, and how do they extend beyond the reductive translation of "suffering"?

2. What are the defining structural features of contemporary modernity that give rise to pervasive dissatisfaction, and how are these characterised in critical social philosophy?

3. How does each dimension of the three-fold typology of dukkha (dukkha-dukkha, vipariṇāma-dukkha, saṅkhāra-dukkha) map onto specific conditions of modern dissatisfaction?

4. How do the Buddhist concepts of *taṇhā* (craving) and *paṭiccasamuppāda* (dependent origination) illuminate the causal mechanisms by which modern institutions perpetuate cycles of dissatisfaction?

5. What are the philosophical implications of this cross-traditional analysis for both Buddhist philosophy and the philosophy of modernity?

## 1.6 Significance of the Study

This research contributes to scholarship in several important ways:

### 1.6.1 Contribution to Buddhist Philosophy
The study demonstrates the continuing philosophical vitality and relevance of classical Buddhist concepts by showing that dukkha, when understood in its full canonical depth, is not merely a historical or soteriological doctrine but a living philosophical framework capable of illuminating contemporary phenomena. This counters the tendency — criticised by Žižek (2003) among others — to reduce Buddhism to either an exotic relic or a therapeutic technique.

### 1.6.2 Contribution to the Philosophy of Modernity
By bringing Buddhist philosophical categories to bear on the diagnosis of modernity, the study offers analytical tools that complement and, in certain respects, surpass the diagnostic capacity of Western critical theory. In particular, the concept of *saṅkhāra-dukkha* — the dissatisfaction inherent in the very nature of conditioned existence — provides a depth of ontological analysis that has no precise equivalent in Western social philosophy, which tends to locate the source of dissatisfaction in specific institutional arrangements rather than in the structural features of conditioned existence itself.

### 1.6.3 Contribution to Comparative Philosophy
The study models a genuinely *comparative* philosophical methodology — one that does not merely juxtapose Eastern and Western concepts but deploys Buddhist categories as active analytical tools for examining phenomena typically analysed through Western frameworks alone. This advances the field of comparative philosophy beyond mere description toward genuine philosophical engagement.

### 1.6.4 Contribution to the Field of Eastern Philosophy
For the field of Eastern Philosophy specifically, this research demonstrates that classical Buddhist philosophical categories are not museum pieces to be studied from a historical distance but rigorous intellectual tools that can be brought into dialogue with contemporary philosophical problems — a contribution that strengthens the case for the continuing academic and intellectual significance of Eastern philosophical traditions.

## 1.7 Scope and Delimitations

### 1.7.1 Scope
This thesis focuses on:

- **The Theravāda and Mādhyamika philosophical traditions** as the primary sources for the analysis of dukkha. The Theravāda tradition (Pāli Canon, Abhidhamma, Visuddhimagga) provides the foundational typology, while Nāgārjuna's Mādhyamika philosophy provides the critical-dialectical dimension (the relationship between dukkha, śūnyatā, and the critique of essentialist thinking).

- **Contemporary modernity** understood primarily as the condition of late-capitalist, technologically advanced, globalised societies. The analysis engages with structural features of modernity rather than the specific conditions of any single nation-state.

- **Philosophical analysis** as distinct from empirical social science. While empirical data (e.g., on rates of depression, anxiety, and dissatisfaction) is referenced to establish the phenomena under investigation, the primary mode of analysis is philosophical.

### 1.7.2 Delimitations

- The thesis does **not** engage extensively with Yogācāra (Cittamātra) Buddhist philosophy, except where it is directly relevant to the phenomenological analysis of dukkha. This delimitation is necessitated by the scope of an MPhil thesis.

- The thesis does **not** address Buddhist meditation practice or mindfulness-based interventions except as objects of philosophical critique (particularly in relation to the "McMindfulness" phenomenon identified by Purser, 2019). The focus is on *philosophical* analysis, not therapeutic application.

- The thesis does **not** claim to provide a comprehensive Buddhist social theory or a Buddhist political philosophy. It is a *diagnostic* philosophical analysis — concerned with understanding and characterising the conditions of dissatisfaction, not with prescribing comprehensive social solutions.

- The thesis engages with **selected** modern critical theorists (Bauman, Han, Rosa, Loy) rather than attempting an exhaustive survey of all Western analyses of modernity. These thinkers are selected for the philosophical resonance of their analyses with Buddhist categories.

## 1.8 Methodology

### 1.8.1 Philosophical Approach: Buddhist Philosophical Analysis Applied to Contemporary Phenomena

The methodology of this thesis is best characterised as **Buddhist philosophical analysis applied to contemporary phenomena** — a form of applied comparative philosophy that deploys Buddhist philosophical categories as diagnostic tools for examining the conditions of modernity.

This methodology involves three interconnected dimensions:

#### i. Textual-Philosophical Analysis (Canonical Exegesis)

The first dimension involves rigorous philosophical analysis of the primary Buddhist canonical and commentarial sources on dukkha. This includes:

- **Primary Pāli Canon texts:** The *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta* (SN 56.11), relevant suttas from the *Saṃyutta Nikāya* (particularly the *Sacca-saṃyutta*, SN 56) and *Majjhima Nikāya*, and the *Mahāsatipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (DN 22).

- **Abhidhamma literature:** Particularly the *Dhammasaṅgaṇī* and *Vibhaṅga*, which provide the phenomenological micro-analysis of mental states relevant to dukkha.

- **Commentarial literature:** The *Visuddhimagga* of Buddhaghosa, which offers the most systematic Theravāda philosophical treatment of dukkha within the framework of dependent origination.

- **Mahāyāna philosophical texts:** Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (particularly chapters XXIV–XXV), analysed through Jay Garfield's (1994) translation and philosophical commentary, and Śāntideva's *Bodhicaryāvatāra* for the ethical dimensions of dukkha.

The aim of this textual-philosophical analysis is not merely historical or descriptive but **systematic** — to extract the philosophical structure, typological distinctions, and analytical categories that the Buddhist tradition has developed for understanding dukkha, and to present them with sufficient philosophical precision that they can be deployed as diagnostic tools.

#### ii. Critical Phenomenological Analysis of Modernity

The second dimension involves a critical phenomenological characterisation of the conditions of contemporary modernity that constitute the phenomena to be diagnosed. This draws on:

- **Zygmunt Bauman's** analysis of "liquid modernity" (2000) — the radical impermanence, instability, and fluidity of modern social forms, relationships, and identities.

- **Byung-Chul Han's** analysis of the "burnout society" (2015) and the "achievement subject" — the pathologies generated by the modern paradigm of performance, self-optimisation, and unlimited possibility.

- **Hartmut Rosa's** analysis of "social acceleration" (2013) — the temporal structures of modernity that generate alienation through the acceleration of the pace of life, technological change, and social change.

- **David Loy's** Buddhist-inflected analysis of the "sense of lack" (1996) — the existential emptiness at the core of modern consumer culture.

This analysis establishes the *explanandum* — the phenomena of modern dissatisfaction that require philosophical diagnosis.

#### iii. Diagnostic Application (Cross-Traditional Analysis)

The third and central dimension of the methodology is the **diagnostic application** of Buddhist philosophical categories to the phenomena of modernity. This involves:

- **Mapping the three-fold typology of dukkha** onto specific conditions of modernity, demonstrating how each dimension (dukkha-dukkha, vipariṇāma-dukkha, saṅkhāra-dukkha) illuminates distinct aspects of contemporary dissatisfaction.

- **Applying the framework of paṭiccasamuppāda** (dependent origination) to analyse the causal mechanisms by which modern institutional structures perpetuate cycles of dissatisfaction.

- **Deploying the analysis of taṇhā** (craving) — in its three-fold form of *kāma-taṇhā* (craving for sensual pleasure), *bhava-taṇhā* (craving for existence/becoming), and *vibhava-taṇhā* (craving for non-existence/annihilation) — to illuminate the motivational dynamics that sustain modern consumer, digital, and achievement culture.

This diagnostic application is not a mere juxtaposition or superficial comparison. It is a genuine philosophical analysis that uses Buddhist categories to *reveal* structural features of modernity that might otherwise remain obscured.

### 1.8.2 Methodological Principles

The methodology is guided by the following principles:

1. **Philosophical Rigour:** All Buddhist concepts are presented with precision and fidelity to the canonical and commentarial sources, avoiding the popularised or diluted versions that characterise much contemporary engagement with Buddhism.

2. **Hermeneutic Charity:** The Buddhist philosophical tradition is treated as a sophisticated intellectual tradition whose categories are taken seriously as potential bearers of philosophical truth, not merely as objects of historical or anthropological curiosity.

3. **Critical Engagement:** The analysis does not uncritically assume the adequacy of Buddhist categories but critically evaluates their diagnostic power, acknowledging limitations and areas where the analysis may be incomplete or require supplementation.

4. **Comparative Integrity:** The comparative dimension of the analysis maintains the integrity of both the Buddhist and Western philosophical traditions, avoiding the temptation to force artificial equivalences or to subordinate one tradition to the other.

### 1.8.3 Note on Translations and Terminology

Throughout this thesis, Pāli terms are used for concepts drawn from the Theravāda tradition (e.g., *dukkha*, *taṇhā*, *paṭiccasamuppāda*, *nibbāna*), and Sanskrit terms are used for concepts drawn from the Mahāyāna tradition (e.g., *duḥkha*, *śūnyatā*, *pratītyasamutpāda*). Where a concept is discussed across both traditions, the Pāli form is used as the default, with the Sanskrit equivalent noted on first occurrence. English translations are provided in parentheses on first use and at key subsequent occurrences. Primary texts are cited from the Pali Text Society (PTS) editions where available, supplemented by Bhikkhu Bodhi's translations for the Saṃyutta Nikāya and Majjhima Nikāya, and by Jay Garfield's translation for Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā*.

## 1.9 Literature Review Summary

A detailed critical review of the literature is presented in Chapter 2. Here, a brief overview of the key strands of existing scholarship is provided to situate the present study.

### 1.9.1 Classical Buddhist Philosophical Sources
The canonical analysis of dukkha begins with the *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta* (SN 56.11) and is developed systematically in the Abhidhamma, the *Visuddhimagga*, and the Mādhyamika philosophy of Nāgārjuna. These sources establish the philosophical foundations — the three-fold typology, the causal framework of dependent origination, and the analysis of craving — that this thesis deploys.

### 1.9.2 Modern Philosophical Interpretations
Key modern interpreters include Walpola Rahula (1959), who established the canonical modern exposition of dukkha; Mark Siderits (2007), who brought analytic philosophical rigour to Buddhist concepts; Bhikkhu Bodhi (1994), who provided precise philosophical mapping of the path to dukkha's cessation; and Peter Harvey (2013), who offered an authoritative systematic overview.

### 1.9.3 Buddhist Philosophy and Modernity
David Loy (1996, 2008, 2015) represents the most sustained scholarly effort to connect Buddhist analysis to modern societal critique. David McMahan (2008) has examined how Buddhist concepts have been transformed in the modern era. Ronald Purser (2019) has critically examined the commodification of Buddhist practice.

### 1.9.4 Western Philosophy of Modernity
The thesis engages with Bauman's "liquid modernity" (2000), Han's "burnout society" (2015), and Rosa's "social acceleration" (2013) as characterisations of the modern condition that resonate with Buddhist philosophical categories.

### 1.9.5 Comparative Philosophy
The comparative dimension draws on Morrison (1997) and Panaïoti (2012) on Buddhism and Nietzsche, Collins (1982) on selfhood, Thompson (2014) on phenomenology, and Flanagan (2011) on Buddhist naturalism.

### 1.9.6 Research Gap
The primary gap identified is the absence of a **systematic philosophical application** of the canonical Buddhist three-fold typology of dukkha as a diagnostic framework for the structural conditions of dissatisfaction in contemporary modernity. This thesis addresses that gap directly.

## 1.10 Definition of Key Terms

| Term (Pāli/Sanskrit) | Translation | Philosophical Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **Dukkha** (दुक्ख) | Suffering, dissatisfaction, unsatisfactoriness | The First Noble Truth; the ontological characterisation of conditioned existence as inherently unsatisfactory. Encompasses three dimensions: dukkha-dukkha, vipariṇāma-dukkha, and saṅkhāra-dukkha. |
| **Dukkha-dukkha** | Suffering of suffering | The first and most obvious dimension: physical and mental pain, illness, grief, lamentation — suffering that is immediately experienced as such. |
| **Vipariṇāma-dukkha** | Suffering of change | The second dimension: the dissatisfaction inherent in the impermanence of all conditioned phenomena, including pleasant experiences, which inevitably change and pass away. |
| **Saṅkhāra-dukkha** | Suffering of conditioned existence | The third and deepest dimension: the unsatisfactoriness inherent in the very nature of conditioned existence itself — the fact that all compounded phenomena (*saṅkhārā*) are by their nature impermanent, dependently arisen, and devoid of enduring selfhood. |
| **Taṇhā** (तण्हा) / **Tṛṣṇā** | Craving, thirst | The Second Noble Truth identifies taṇhā as the origination (*samudaya*) of dukkha. Three forms: *kāma-taṇhā* (craving for sensual pleasure), *bhava-taṇhā* (craving for existence/becoming), *vibhava-taṇhā* (craving for non-existence). |
| **Paṭiccasamuppāda** / **Pratītyasamutpāda** | Dependent origination | The twelve-link causal chain explaining how dukkha arises through the mutual conditioning of ignorance, formations, consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense bases, contact, feeling, craving, clinging, becoming, birth, and ageing-and-death. |
| **Anicca** (अनिच्च) / **Anitya** | Impermanence | One of the Three Marks of Existence; the universal characteristic of all conditioned phenomena that they are transient, unstable, and subject to arising and passing away. |
| **Anattā** (अनत्ता) / **Anātman** | Non-self | One of the Three Marks of Existence; the absence of a permanent, unchanging self or essence in any phenomenon, including persons. |
| **Saṅkhāra** (सङ्खार) / **Saṃskāra** | Formations, conditioned things | All compounded, conditioned phenomena — everything that arises through causes and conditions. In the context of saṅkhāra-dukkha, refers to the totality of conditioned existence. |
| **Nirodha** (निरोध) | Cessation | The Third Noble Truth: the cessation of dukkha through the cessation of taṇhā. In this thesis, examined philosophically as the possibility of a genuine response to modern dissatisfaction. |
| **Śūnyatā** (शून्यता) | Emptiness | Nāgārjuna's central concept: all phenomena are empty of inherent existence (*svabhāva*), existing only through dependent origination. The philosophical link between śūnyatā and dukkha is central to the Mādhyamika analysis. |
| **Modernity** | — | Used in this thesis to denote the contemporary condition of late-capitalist, technologically advanced, globalised societies, characterised by what Bauman calls "liquidity," Han calls "achievement culture," and Rosa calls "social acceleration." |

## 1.11 Organisation of the Thesis

The thesis is organised into nine chapters:

**Chapter 1: Introduction** (the present chapter) establishes the background, problem statement, thesis statement, research objectives and questions, significance, scope, methodology, and key definitions.

**Chapter 2: Literature Review** provides a detailed critical review of the relevant scholarship across canonical Buddhist sources, modern philosophical interpretations of dukkha, Buddhist philosophy and modernity, Western philosophy of modernity, and comparative philosophy, culminating in the identification and justification of the research gap.

**Chapter 3: The Philosophy of Dukkha** presents a systematic philosophical analysis of dukkha drawing on the Pāli Canon, Abhidhamma, Visuddhimagga, and Nāgārjuna's Mādhyamika philosophy, with particular attention to the three-fold typology and its ontological, phenomenological, and ethical dimensions.

**Chapter 4: The Condition of Modernity** characterises the structural features of contemporary modernity that give rise to pervasive dissatisfaction, drawing on Bauman, Han, Rosa, and Loy, and establishing the phenomena that require philosophical diagnosis.

**Chapter 5: Dukkha-Dukkha in Modernity** applies the first dimension of dukkha to the obvious and direct forms of suffering in contemporary society — mental health crises, inequality, existential anxiety, and the suffering generated by modern institutional structures.

**Chapter 6: Vipariṇāma-Dukkha and Liquid Modernity** applies the second dimension of dukkha to the suffering generated by the radical impermanence and instability of modern social forms, relationships, identities, and institutions, drawing particularly on Bauman's analysis of "liquid modernity."

**Chapter 7: Saṅkhāra-Dukkha and the Structural Conditions of Dissatisfaction** applies the deepest and most philosophically significant dimension of dukkha to the structural features of modernity that generate dissatisfaction not through specific misfortunes but through the very nature of the modern conditioned existence — consumerism, digital culture, the achievement-performance paradigm, and the crisis of meaning.

**Chapter 8: Taṇhā, Paṭiccasamuppāda, and the Mechanisms of Modern Craving** analyses the causal mechanisms of modern dissatisfaction through the Buddhist frameworks of dependent origination and craving, mapping the three forms of taṇhā to specific features of contemporary culture, and asking whether the Buddhist analysis of *nirodha* (cessation) offers any genuine philosophical insight into transcending modern dukkha.

**Chapter 9: Conclusion** summarises the findings, evaluates the thesis statement, discusses the implications for Buddhist philosophy and the philosophy of modernity, acknowledges limitations, and suggests directions for further research.
